## Accounting: The Language of the Business World

Accounting is introduced as the "language of the business world" [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-1, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-12]. It helps in understanding company fundamentals, revenue changes, funding sources, and valuation methods, as demonstrated through analyses of recent business news such as Amazon's acquisition of Roomba and Berkshire Hathaway's earnings and investment losses [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-1].

### The Need for Accounting: Andy's AI Company
To illustrate the importance of accounting, the lecture presents a scenario: Andy, an NUS graduate, needs $10 million to grow his AI company [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-11]. To convince investors, banks, or venture capitalists, he needs reliable financial statements that articulate his company's resources, obligations, and profitability [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-2, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-12].

### Definition of Accounting
Accounting is defined as the system used to **identify, record, and communicate quantitative information**, primarily financial in nature, about a business organization that is useful for decision-making [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-12].

### Users of Accounting Information
Financial statements serve various users, both external and internal [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-14]:

*   **External Users**:
    *   **Shareholders and potential investors**: Interested in dividends and potential for high returns (buy low, sell high).
    *   **Creditors**: Assess a company's ability to repay loans.
    *   **Suppliers**: Evaluate a company's ability to pay for goods/services and maintain demand.
    *   **Customers**: Ensure reliable product delivery and supply.
    *   **Competitors**: Analyze an "enemy's" performance and strategy.
    *   **Regulators**: Oversee market fairness and compliance.
    *   **Taxing Authorities**: Collect appropriate levels of taxes.

*   **Internal Users**:
    *   **Managers and employees**: Plan operations, seek fair compensation, and opportunities for promotion.

### Types of Accounting Systems
There are three main types of accounting systems [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-15]:
1.  **Financial Accounting System**: Reports economic information to **external users** (investors, creditors, suppliers, customers) through periodic financial statements and disclosures. This course focuses primarily on Financial Accounting.
2.  **Managerial Accounting System**: Reports economic information to **internal users** (internal decision-makers, managers) through detailed plans and continuous performance reports.
3.  **Tax Accounting**: Reports information to tax authorities.

The primary objective of external financial reporting is to provide useful economic information about a business to help external parties, mainly investors and creditors, make sound financial decisions [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-15].

### Types of Business Entities
Businesses can be structured in different ways, each with implications for ownership and liability [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-16]:

*   **Sole Proprietorship**: Owned by a single person with unlimited liability.
*   **Partnership**: Owned by two or more partners with unlimited liability, shared among partners.
*   **Corporation**: Owned by many holders through shares (shareholders). It is a separate legal entity, meaning shareholders are *not* personally liable for the corporation's acts; they have limited liability.

## Financial Accounting Standards

The content of financial statements is determined by accounting standards [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-17].

### Standard Setters
*   **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)**: Issues International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-17, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-18].
*   **Singapore Financial Reporting Standards (FRS)**: In Singapore, companies must follow FRS, which is based on IFRS. The FRS are set by the Accounting Standards Council (ASC) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-17, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-18]. The terms IFRS and IAS (International Accounting Standards, which were older IFRS issuances) can be used interchangeably [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-17].
*   **U.S. Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**: In the U.S., standards are set by the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-18].

There is a global convergence towards IFRS to enhance comparability of accounting reports worldwide. US GAAP and IFRS are also pursuing a convergence process [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-19].

### IASB Conceptual Framework
The Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting (revised 2018) is the starting point for developing IFRS standards. It defines the objective of financial reporting (providing useful information), outlines basic accounting concepts, and provides guidelines for good accounting, particularly through **Qualitative Characteristics** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-20].

#### Qualitative Characteristics of Accounting Information
Qualitative characteristics define the types of information most useful to users [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-20].

**Fundamental Qualitative Characteristics** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-21]:
1.  **Relevance**: Financial information is relevant if it can influence users' decisions. It has:
    *   **Predictive value**: Helps users forecast future outcomes.
    *   **Confirmatory value**: Confirms or corrects past evaluations.
2.  **Faithful Representation**: Financial information must represent the economic phenomena it purports to represent. It requires information to be:
    *   **True, fair, and neutral**: Unbiased in presentation.
    *   **Supported by prudence**: Exercising caution when making judgments under uncertainty.

**Enhancing Qualitative Characteristics** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-22]:
1.  **Comparability**: Allows users to identify and understand similarities and differences among items. This enables comparison between different companies or a company's performance across different periods.
2.  **Verifiability**: Different knowledgeable and independent observers can reach a consensus that a particular depiction is a faithful representation. This means numbers can be checked for correctness.
3.  **Timeliness**: Information is available to decision-makers in time to be capable of influencing their decisions. It should not be stale or out of date.
4.  **Understandability**: Information is classified, characterized, and presented clearly and concisely, allowing users with a reasonable knowledge of business and economic activities to understand it.

## Ethical Conduct in Accounting
Ethics – beliefs that distinguish right from wrong – are crucial in accounting. For accounting information to be useful, it must be trusted by its users, and accountants are responsible for preventing misrepresentation [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-23]. The lecture highlights several accounting scandals (e.g., Luckin Coffee, Hin Leong, Satyam, Enron, WorldCom) as examples of the consequences of greed and poor ethics, emphasizing that "Good ethics are good business!" [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-23].

## Auditors & External Audit
An **external audit** involves examining financial reports and underlying transactions to ensure compliance with accounting standards [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-24]. Financial statements are the responsibility of a company's management [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-24].

An **auditor** is an independent certified public accountant (CPA) who performs an external audit and issues an audit report containing an auditing opinion on whether the financial statements comply with prevailing accounting standards [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-24]. While an audit report does not guarantee absolute accuracy, it provides additional assurance that the financial statements are not misleading and can be relied upon by the public [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-24]. Prominent audit firms include the "Big Four": Deloitte, PwC, EY, and KPMG [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-24].

## Accounting & Technology
Technology has significantly enhanced accounting by automating routine bookkeeping, enabling easy gathering of vast transaction data, compiling large data amounts quickly and accurately, and assisting in analyzing financial information [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-25]. However, technology has *not* replaced human judgment, which is still needed to input useful information and analyze data for sound decision-making [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-25].

## The Fundamental Accounting Equation

The fundamental accounting equation is **Assets = Liabilities + Equity** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-27]. This concept was developed by Luca Pacioli, a Renaissance-era monk [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-27]. It signifies that the claims on a company's assets (total claims) are equal to the assets themselves. These claims are split between creditors (Liabilities) and owners (Equity) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-27]. The equation **always balances** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-27].

### Defining Components of the Accounting Equation (IASB Conceptual Framework)

*   **Assets**: A resource controlled by the entity as a result of a **past event**, from which **future economic benefits** are expected to flow to the entity [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-28].
    *   Examples: Cash, Accounts Receivable, Inventory, Buildings [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-28].

*   **Liabilities**: A **present obligation** of the entity arising from **past events**, the settlement of which is expected to result in an **outflow of resources** embodying economic benefits [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-29]. These are creditors' claims on assets.
    *   Examples: Accounts Payable, Income Taxes Payable, Mortgage Payable, Unearned Revenue [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-29].
    *   A potential damage from a lawsuit is a liability if the likelihood of paying is high, representing a current obligation from a past event [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-4].

*   **Equity**: The owner's claim on the residual interest in the assets after deducting all liabilities, representing the net assets (total assets – total liabilities) of the company [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-30]. Claims arise from:
    1.  **Capital Stock / Share Capital**: Original equity invested by owners.
    2.  **Retained Earnings**: Equity earned by the company, representing accumulated earnings not distributed to owners. Profits (Revenues - Expenses) increase retained earnings, while Dividends (portion of profits distributed to shareholders) decrease them [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-30].
    3.  **Other Equity**: (e.g., Reserves, used to track Other Comprehensive Income items).

*   **Income (Revenue, Gains)**: An inflow or enhancement of assets (or a decrease in liabilities) that results in an **increase in equity** during the accounting period, other than contributions from owners [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-31].
    *   Receiving a gift of a building is considered income as it enhances an asset and increases equity [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-3].

*   **Expense**: An outflow or depletion of assets (or an incurrence of liabilities) that results in a **decrease in equity** during the accounting period, other than distributions to owners [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-31].
    *   Repaying loan principal is a settlement of an existing liability and does not affect equity, thus not an expense [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-3]. Buying back shares (Treasury Shares) is an equity transaction, not an expense [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-3].

*   **Capital Maintenance Adjustments**: Revaluation or restatement of assets/liabilities resulting in changes in equity that are *not* included in the income statement. These are treated as **Other Comprehensive Income (OCI)** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-31].

**Example**: If a company has total assets of $50,000 and liabilities of $30,000, the owners' claim (equity) is $20,000 ($50,000 - $30,000) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-3].

## The Four Major Financial Statements

FRS (IAS) 1 states that a complete set of financial statements comprises four major statements [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-32]:

1.  Statement of Financial Position (SFP) (also known as Balance Sheet)
2.  Statement of Comprehensive Income (SCI) (can include a Statement of Profit & Loss (SPL) / Income Statement (IS))
3.  Statement of Changes in Equity (SCE)
4.  Statement of Cash Flows (SCF)

### 1. Statement of Financial Position (SFP) / Balance Sheet
The SFP shows a company's assets, liabilities, and equity at a **particular point in time**, akin to a camera snapshot [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-33, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-34, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-46].

The general format of a Classified Statement of Financial Position includes [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-33]:
*   **Assets**: Resources that will provide future benefits. Classified by liquidity:
    *   **Current Assets**: To be used or turned into cash within one year (e.g., cash, receivables, inventory).
    *   **Noncurrent Assets**: To be used for more than one year (e.g., long-term investments, fixed assets, intangible assets).
*   **Liabilities**: Obligations to be settled in the future. Classified by resolution period:
    *   **Current Liabilities**: To be resolved within one year (e.g., accounts payable, salaries payable, unearned revenue).
    *   **Noncurrent Liabilities**: Long-term obligations (e.g., long-term debt, mortgages payable).
*   **Equity**: Owner's claim to the residual interest.
    *   **Capital Stock / Share Capital**: Original investment by owners.
    *   **Retained Earnings**: Accumulated earnings not distributed.

**Limitations of the SFP**:
*   Assets are recorded at **cost**, not market value. Book value (value of equity on books) is usually less than market value (market price per share x number of shares) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-35].
*   Not all economic assets (e.g., company's reputation, in-house brand) are reflected [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-35].

### 2. Statement of Comprehensive Income (SCI)
The SCI shows a firm's economic performance over a **period of time**, like a movie capturing economic activities [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-36, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-37, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-46].

*   **Net Income (NI)** = Revenues – Expenses [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-36, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-46]. Net income increases equity (retained earnings), while a net loss decreases it [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-36]. NI is often referred to as the "bottom line" [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-37].
*   **Other Comprehensive Income (OCI)** includes revaluation or restatement of assets/liabilities that result in changes in equity but are not revenues or expenses [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-36]. OCI affects equity, not net income [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-36].
*   **Comprehensive Income** = Net Income + OCI [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-36, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-46].

IAS 1 allows companies to present Comprehensive Income either as a single statement or as two separate statements: an Income Statement (or Statement of Profit & Loss) and a Statement of Comprehensive Income [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-36].

#### Classifying the Income Statement
*   **Revenues vs. Gains/Losses**:
    *   **Revenue**: Income earned from the **normal operations** of the business (appears at the top of the income statement) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-38].
    *   **Gain/Loss**: Money made or lost from activities **outside the normal business** operations (appears at the bottom after operating income) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-38].
*   **Operating vs. Non-Operating Categories**:
    *   **Operating Expenses**: Incurred in the normal operation of the business (e.g., salaries, rent, depreciation) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-38].
    *   **Non-Operating Revenues/Expenses**: No connection with the specific nature of the business operations (e.g., interest revenue/expense, tax expense) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-38].

### 3. Statement of Changes in Equity (SCE)
The SCE shows how equity changes over a reporting period [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-39, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-46]. Equity is comprised of:
*   **Share Capital**: Amount invested by owners.
*   **Retained Earnings (RE)**: Accumulated earnings not distributed.
    *   Ending RE = Beginning RE + Net Income – Dividends [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-39].
*   **Other Equity**: (e.g., Reserves, used to track OCI items).
*   Beginning Equity + Net Increase/(decrease) in Capital + Net Income – Dividends + OCI = Ending Equity [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-40, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-46].

### 4. Statement of Cash Flows (SCF)
The SCF shows a company's cash inflows and outflows over a **period of time** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-41, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-46]. It is divided into three elements:
1.  **Cash Flows from Operating Activities (CFO)**: Cash flows directly related to the company's main business operations that generate revenue (e.g., payments from customers, payments to suppliers, salaries, taxes) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-41].
2.  **Cash Flows from Investing Activities (CFI)**: Cash flows related to the acquisition or sale of a company's productive assets (e.g., buying/selling equipment, land, buildings) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-41].
3.  **Cash Flows from Financing Activities (CFF)**: Cash flows related to the financing of the company (e.g., receiving money from investors/creditors, paying loans/dividends) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-41].

The overall **Change in Cash = CFO + CFI + CFF** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-41, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-46].

### Notes to Financial Statements
Notes to financial statements provide supplemental information about a company's financial conditions and economic performance and are an integral part of the statements. They typically include [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-42]:
1.  Summary of Significant Accounting Policies.
2.  Additional Information about the Summary Totals.
3.  Disclosure of Information Not Recognized.
4.  Supplementary Information (required by the IASB).

### Relationships Among the Four Financial Statements
The four financial statements are interconnected [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-44]:
*   **Net Income** from the Income Statement is a component in determining **Ending Retained Earnings** in the Statement of Changes in Equity.
*   The **Ending Equity (including Retained Earnings)** from the Statement of Changes in Equity is reported in the **Equity section** of the Statement of Financial Position.
*   The **Ending Cash Balance** from the Statement of Cash Flows is reported as the **Cash asset** in the Statement of Financial Position.

## Fundamental Concepts & Assumptions of Accounting (Appendix A)

Several fundamental concepts and assumptions underpin accounting [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-01-slides-accounting-in-business-and-fs-overview-pdf#section-50]:
*   **Separate Entity Concept**: The activities of a business entity are kept separate from its individual owners.
*   **Time-Period Assumption**: A business entity's activities can be divided into specific time periods (e.g., monthly, quarterly, annually) for reporting.
*   **Assumption of Arm's-Length Transactions**: Business dealings occur between independent and rational parties acting in their own self-interest (i.e., no bias).
*   **Cost Principle**: Transactions are recorded at their historical cost, which is the original amount paid or received, representing the fair market value at the time of the transaction.
*   **Fair Value Principle**: Some assets and liabilities are measured at fair value to improve the relevance of accounting information (IFRS allows this for certain items).
*   **Monetary Measurement Concept**: Only economic activities measurable in monetary terms are recorded in the accounting system.
*   **Going Concern Assumption**: It is assumed that a business entity will continue to exist in the foreseeable future.

## Key Takeaways
*   Accounting is the language of business, used by both external and internal decision-makers.
*   Financial accounting provides useful economic information to help external parties make financial decisions.
*   Accounting is guided by ethics, principles, and standards (IASB → IFRS; Singapore uses FRS based on IFRS).
*   Qualitative characteristics of accounting information include Relevance, Faithful Representation (fundamental), and Comparability, Verifiability, Timeliness, Understandability (enhancing).
*   The Fundamental Accounting Equation: **Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders' Equity** (always balances).
*   The four major Financial Statements are the Statement of Financial Position (SFP), Statement of Comprehensive Income (SCI), Statement of Changes in Equity (SCE), and Statement of Cash Flows (SCF).
*   SFP provides a snapshot (Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity).
*   SCI shows performance over a period (Revenues – Expenses = Net Income; Net Income + OCI = Comprehensive Income).
*   SCE details changes in equity over a period (Beg Equity + Net Increase/Decrease in Capital + Net Income – Dividends + OCI = End Equity).
*   SCF reports cash movements over a period (Changes in Cash = CFO + CFI + CFF).
