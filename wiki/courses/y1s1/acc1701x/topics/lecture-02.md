# Lecture 02: The Accounting Cycle: Mechanics of Accounting

## Lecture 02: The Accounting Cycle: Mechanics of Accounting

This lecture deepens the understanding of the accounting process, moving from the fundamental concepts introduced in [[lecture-01|Lecture 01: ACC 1701X Accounting for Decision Makers]] to the mechanics of recording transactions and preparing preliminary financial reports. It covers the definition of transactions, the steps in the accounting cycle, the double-entry accounting system using debits and credits, and the preparation of a trial balance. Finally, it introduces key financial ratios like Return on Assets (ROA) and Debt Ratio for basic financial analysis [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-4].

### Prior Class Refresher

From [[lecture-01|Lecture 01]], we recall the fundamental accounting equation and the basic financial statements [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-1]:

*   **Fundamental Accounting Equation**:
    $$ \text{ASSETS} = \text{LIABILITIES} + \text{EQUITY} $$
    [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-1]

*   **Basic Financial Statements**:
    1.  **Statement of Financial Position (SFP)**, also known as the Balance Sheet: Reflects the accounting equation (Assets = Liabilities + Equity) at a specific point in time [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-1].
    2.  **Statement of Profit & Loss (SPL)**, also known as the Income Statement: Reports net income (Net Income = Revenues – Expenses + Gain/(Loss)) over a period [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-1].
    3.  **Statement of Changes in Equity (SCE)**: Shows how equity changes over a period (Beginning Equity + Net Increase in Capital + Net Income – Dividends + Other Comprehensive Income (OCI) = Ending Equity) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-1].
    4.  **Statement of Cash Flows (SCF)**: Reports changes in cash (Changes in Cash = Cash Flow from Operating Activities (CFO) + Cash Flow from Investing Activities (CFI) + Cash Flow from Financing Activities (CFF)) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-1].

### Understanding Business Transactions

A core concept in accounting is the "transaction." Transactions are past events that have a measurable economic impact on a company [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-5]. They can be categorized as:

*   **External events**: Exchanges of assets, services, or liabilities between a firm and other external parties. For example, Best Denki buying computers from Lenovo for cash involves an exchange of one asset (cash) for another (computer) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-5].
*   **Internal events**: Events that are not exchanges with external parties but directly affect the accounting entity. An example is an unexpected fire destroying a factory, leading to a loss of an asset and a reduction in equity [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-5].

**Important Notes on Transactions**:
*   An event is *not* a transaction if the exchange has not yet occurred (e.g., signing a contract for a service is not a transaction until the service has been rendered) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-5].
*   Events that cannot be reliably measured in monetary terms cannot be recorded in the accounting system and, therefore, will not be reflected in the financial statements [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-5].

**Real-world examples of transaction characteristics**:
Different companies will have different predominant asset or liability characteristics based on their business model:
*   **Ikea**, a retail company, held almost US$3.9 billion in inventory in 2021, representing 17.5% of its total assets. This is typical for a business focused on selling inventory [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-02-supplement-pdf#section-1].
*   **DBS**, a bank, had about US$299 billion in receivables in 2021, accounting for 59% of its total assets. Banks primarily earn by loaning money and collecting interest, making "Loans Receivable" a significant asset [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-02-supplement-pdf#section-1].
*   **ExxonMobil** had about US$217 billion in Property, Plant & Equipment (PPE) in 2021, which was about 64% of its total assets. This is characteristic of capital-intensive industries like refineries and oil platforms [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-02-supplement-pdf#section-1].
*   **Singapore Airlines** had about US$1.1 billion in a current liability related to services for which customers had already paid but had not yet been delivered (unearned revenue), representing about 26% of its current liabilities. Airlines frequently pre-sell tickets [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-02-supplement-pdf#section-1].

### The Accounting Cycle: How Transactions are Recorded

The accounting cycle is the procedure for analyzing, recording, summarizing, and reporting the transactions of a business [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-7]. It involves the following steps:

1.  **Analyze Transaction**:
    *   Identify and describe events using source documents (e.g., sales receipts, purchase orders, invoices from suppliers) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-6].
2.  **Record Transaction in a Journal**:
    *   Transactions are recorded chronologically in a general journal [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-6].
3.  **Post Journals to Ledger Accounts**:
    *   Information from journals is transferred to individual ledger accounts at the end of an accounting period. The ledger houses all different types of accounts (e.g., cash, accounts payable, equipment, sales revenue) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-6].
4.  **Prepare Trial Balance**:
    *   A listing of all accounts with their balances to verify that total debits equal total credits [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-6].
5.  **Use Trial Balance to Prepare Financial Statements (FS)**:
    *   The trial balance serves as the basis for preparing the Statement of Financial Position, Income Statement, and Statement of Changes in Equity [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-6].

*(The subsequent steps of the accounting cycle, including adjusting entries, adjusted trial balance, closing entries, and post-closing trial balance, will be covered in [[lecture-03|Lecture 03: (Adjusting Accounts)]] and [[lecture-04|Lecture 04: Completing Accounting Cycle]])* [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-7, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-58].

### Understanding Accounts, Ledgers, and Chart of Accounts

An **account** is a record that keeps track of activities by recording increases and decreases in specific items [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-8]. Account types are based on the fundamental accounting equation: assets, liabilities, equity, revenue, and expense [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-8]. Think of an individual account as a summary of every transaction affecting that particular item [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-8].

All accounts together make up the **General Ledger**, which is a record containing all accounts used by the company [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-8].

A **Chart of Accounts** is a comprehensive list of all accounts used by a company, along with individual unique identifying account numbers (e.g., 101 Cash, 102 Accounts Receivable, 201 Accounts Payable) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-8].

### The Double-Entry System: Debits and Credits

The "power and beauty of accounting" lies in the **double-entry system**, where each transaction affects at least two accounts [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-10]. This reflects the "duality of effect" notion: for every exchange, the business entity gives up something and receives something in return [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-10].

The accounting equation (Assets = Liabilities + Equity) must *always* balance after every transaction [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-10].

**Debits (Dr)** are entries on the **left side** of an account, and **Credits (Cr)** are entries on the **right side** of an account, typically represented by a "T-account" format [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-11]. Every business transaction involves at least one debit and one credit, and total debits must always equal total credits for each transaction [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-11].

The type of account determines how increases and decreases are recorded using debits and credits [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-12]:

| Account Type           | Normal Balance | To Increase (+) | To Decrease (-) |
| :--------------------- | :------------- | :-------------- | :-------------- |
| **Assets**             | Debit          | Debit           | Credit          |
| **Liabilities**        | Credit         | Credit          | Debit           |
| **Equity**             | Credit         | Credit          | Debit           |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Share Capital | Credit         | Credit          | Debit           |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dividends      | Debit          | Debit           | Credit          |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Revenues       | Credit         | Credit          | Debit           |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Expenses       | Debit          | Debit           | Credit          |

[source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-12, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-13]

**Key rules for debit and credit**:
*   Cash increases on the debit side [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-02-supplement-pdf#section-2].
*   Office equipment increases on the debit side [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-02-supplement-pdf#section-2].
*   An increase to a revenue account is a credit [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-02-supplement-pdf#section-2].
*   Accounts with a normal credit balance include Notes Payable, Accounts Payable, Share Capital, and Revenues [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-02-supplement-pdf#section-2, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-12, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-13].

### The Accounting Cycle in Action: The Apple Story (1976)

Let's illustrate the accounting procedures with the founding of Apple Computer by Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak in 1976 [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-19].

#### 1. Analyze Transactions

**Events #1-3: Inspiration, Cost Estimation, and Big Order (March 1975 - April 1976)**
*   Wozniak and Jobs got inspired at a computer club.
*   Wozniak estimated design costs and selling prices for computers.
*   Jobs received a big order for 100 assembled sets, with payment cash on delivery [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-20].

**Transaction Analysis**: None of these events are recorded as transactions. Why? Because there were no actual exchanges of goods or services yet [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-20].

**Event #4: Initial Investment and Debt (April 1976 - Starting Up)**
Wozniak sold his calculator for $500, Jobs sold his VW bus for $800, and they borrowed $5,000 from a friend to start Apple [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-21].

| ASSETS (A) | = | LIABILITIES (L) | + | EQUITY (E)           | Transaction Type      |
| :--------- | :- | :-------------- | :- | :------------------- | :-------------------- |
| + $500 Cash | |                 | | + $500 Share Capital (Wozniak) | Issuance of Shares    |
| + $800 Cash | |                 | | + $800 Share Capital (Jobs) | Issuance of Shares    |
| + $5,000 Cash | | + $5,000 Debt   | |                      | Financing through Debt |
| **$6,300** | = | **$5,000**      | + | **$1,300**           |                       |

The accounting equation remains in balance [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-21].

#### 2. Record Transaction in a Journal

Transactions are recorded chronologically in a general journal. Debit entries are always written first, followed by indented credit entries. Total debits must equal total credits [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-16].

**Event #4 Journal Entry**:
| Date | Account Titles and Explanation | Ref. | Debit | Credit |
| :--- | :----------------------------- | :--- | :---- | :----- |
| (4) | Cash (A)                         |      | 6,300 |        |
|     | &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Share Capital (E) |      |       | 1,300  |
|     | &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Debt (L)           |      |       | 5,000  |
|     | *(Initial investments)*        |      |       |        |

[source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-22]

#### 3. Post Journals to Ledger Accounts (T-Accounts)

After journal entries, information is transferred to individual T-accounts, which summarize increases and decreases for each account [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-17]. The ending balance on a T-account is the difference between total debits and total credits [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-17].

**Event #4 T-Accounts**:
```
         ASSETS                    LIABILITIES                       EQUITY
-----------------------   -----------------------------     -----------------------
         Cash                            Debt                       Share Capital
(4) $1,300                            (4) $5,000                      (4) $1,300
(4) $5,000
```
[source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-22]

### 4. Prepare Trial Balance

A **trial balance** is a list of all accounts with their balances, used to check the equality of total debits and total credits [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-44]. It lists accounts in the order they appear on financial statements: assets, liabilities, stockholders' equity, revenues, and expenses [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-44]. The trial balance is a crucial step in preparing the financial statements [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-44].

**Apple Story Trial Balance (July 31, 1976)**:
Using the ending balances from the T-accounts, a trial balance is prepared [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-45].

| Accounts                | Debit    | Credit   |
| :---------------------- | :------- | :------- |
| Cash                    | 36,800   |          |
| Equipment               | 200      |          |
| Prepaid Rent            | 3,500    |          |
| Long-term Debt          |          | 5,000    |
| Unearned Sales Revenue  |          | 11,000   |
| Share Capital           |          | 1,300    |
| Dividends               | 4,000    |          |
| Sales Revenue           |          | 50,000   |
| Development Expense     | 800      |          |
| Cost of Goods Sold      | 22,000   |          |
| **Total**               | **67,300** | **67,300** |

As always, DEBIT = CREDIT [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-45].

**Common Mistakes in Preparing a Trial Balance**:
If a trial balance does not balance, check the following [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-46]:
1.  Ensure trial balance columns are correctly added.
2.  Verify account balances are correctly entered from the ledger.
3.  Check if debit or credit accounts are mistakenly placed on the wrong side of the trial balance.
4.  Re-compute each account balance in the ledger.
5.  Verify that each journal entry is posted correctly.
6.  Verify that each original journal entry has equal debits and credits.

**Note**: A balanced trial balance does not necessarily mean it is free of all errors (e.g., if a transaction was entirely omitted or recorded with incorrect accounts that still balance) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-46].

### 5. Use Trial Balance to Prepare Financial Statements

The balances from the trial balance are then used to prepare the financial statements [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-47].

**Relationships Among the Four Financial Statements**:
1.  **Income Statement**: [[lecture-01#Statement of Profit & Loss (Income Statement)]] determines **Net Income** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-48].
2.  **Statement of Changes in Equity**: Net Income (from the Income Statement) is a component used to determine **Ending Equity** (specifically, Retained Earnings) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-48].
3.  **Statement of Financial Position**: The Ending Equity (from the Statement of Changes in Equity) and the ending cash balance (from the Statement of Cash Flows) are reported on the Balance Sheet [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-48].
4.  **Statement of Cash Flows**: [[lecture-01#Statement of Cash Flows]] reports changes in cash, with the ending cash balance being reported as an asset on the Statement of Financial Position. This statement provides greater detail on how cash changes, and will be covered in a later part of the course [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-48].

### Financial Statement Analysis (FSA)

Financial Statement Analysis (FSA) involves using financial ratios to aid in decision-making [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-50]. General areas of financial statement analysis include:
1.  **Liquidity and efficiency**: Ability to meet short-term obligations and efficiently generate revenues.
2.  **Solvency**: Ability to meet long-term obligations and generate future revenues.
3.  **Profitability**: Ability to generate attractive and sufficient financial rewards for investors.
4.  **Cash Flow**: Ability to manage cash inflow and outflow ("Cash is King").
5.  **Market prospects**: Ability to generate positive market expectations [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-50].

This module introduces two key ratios: Return on Assets (ROA) and Debt Ratio [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-49].

#### Return on Assets (ROA)

**Return on Assets (ROA)** is a profitability ratio that measures how much net profit a company earns from its assets [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-51]. It is also known as "Return on Investment" [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-51].

The formula for ROA is:
$$ \text{Return on assets} = \frac{\text{Net profit}}{\text{Average total assets}} $$
[source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-51]

ROA is useful for evaluating management performance, analyzing profits, forecasting, and benchmarking against competitors or prior years' performance. A higher ratio indicates a higher return and is generally preferred [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-51].

**Example: ComfortDelGro vs. Uber ROA (2019-2021)**:
Comparing ComfortDelGro and Uber's ROA [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-53]:

|            | **ComfortDelGro** | **Uber**   |
| :--------- | :---------------- | :--------- |
|            | **2021** | **2020** | **2021** | **2020** |
| Net Profit | 160      | 92       | (8,506)  | (6,768)  |
| Avg Total Assets | 4,957    | 5,169    | 36,013   | 32,507   |
| **ROA**    | **0.0323 (3.23%)** | **0.0177 (1.77%)** | **-0.2362 (-23.62%)** | **-0.2082 (-20.82%)** |

*   ComfortDelGro's ROA was positive (1.7% in 2020 and 3.2% in 2021), indicating profitability from its assets [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-53].
*   Uber, however, reported losses in both years, resulting in a negative ROA, indicating unprofitability [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-53].

#### Debt Ratio

The **Debt Ratio** is a solvency ratio that measures the proportion of a company's total liabilities relative to its total assets [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-54]. It is useful for evaluating the level of debt risk [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-54].

The formula for Debt Ratio is:
$$ \text{Debt Ratio} = \frac{\text{Total Liabilities}}{\text{Total Assets}} $$
[source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-54]

Companies finance their assets either through liabilities or equity. A company that uses a lot of liabilities to finance its assets is said to have high **financial leverage** (highly leveraged). A higher debt ratio indicates higher leverage and thus higher risk [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-54].

**Example: ComfortDelGro vs. Uber Debt Ratio (2019-2021)**:
Comparing ComfortDelGro and Uber's Debt Ratio [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-56]:

|                 | **ComfortDelGro** | **Uber**   |
| :-------------- | :---------------- | :--------- |
|                 | **2021** | **2020** | **2019** | **2021** | **2020** | **2019** |
| Total Liabilities | 1,819    | 1,890    | 2,370    | 23,425   | 19,498   | 16,578   |
| Total Assets    | 4,955    | 4,959    | 5,379    | 38,774   | 33,252   | 31,761   |
| **Debt Ratio**  | **0.3670** | **0.3811** | **0.4406** | **0.6041** | **0.5864** | **0.5220** |

*   Uber's debt ratio is significantly higher than ComfortDelGro's, indicating that Uber is more highly leveraged and thus carries a higher level of debt risk [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-56].
*   Uber's debt ratio also shows a trend of increasing from 2019 to 2021, suggesting a continuous increase in financial leverage over time [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-02-slides-mechanics-of-accounting-pdf#section-56].
