# Lecture 03: (Adjusting Accounts)

This page covers the core concepts of adjusting accounts, emphasizing the importance of periodic reporting and accrual-basis accounting in the ACC1701X module. It introduces key accounting principles like the Time Period Assumption, Revenue Recognition Principle, and Matching Principle, and explains how these principles necessitate adjustments at the end of an accounting period. The page also includes a refresher on Financial Statement Analysis (FSA) from Lecture 02, specifically covering Return on Assets (ROA) and Debt Ratio.

    *   Increases in Assets are Debits.

### Why Periodic Reporting?

One of the enhancing qualitative characteristics of financial reporting is **timeliness** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-2]. Regular reports provide timely information, and accounting systems prepare these reports at regular intervals.

This is supported by the **Time Period Assumption**: A business entity's activities can be divided into specific time periods for reporting purposes [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-2]. These periods can be:
*   **Calendar Year**: January 1 to December 31.
*   **Fiscal Year**: Any 12 consecutive months ending on a date other than December 31 (e.g., April 1 - March 31).
*   Other common intervals include quarterly, monthly, semi-annually, or annually [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-2].

### Importance of Accrual Accounting

Accrual accounting is crucial for better measuring a firm's performance compared to cash basis data [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-3].

#### Accrual-Basis Accounting vs. Cash-Basis Accounting

| Feature               | Accrual-Basis Accounting                                                                                                       | Cash-Basis Accounting                                                                                                                                                                    |
| :-------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Event Recognition** | Recognizes an event when its main economic impact occurs.                                                                      | Simply adds up cash inflows and cash outflows.                                                                                                                                           |
| **Revenue Recording** | Revenues are recorded when they are **earned**, regardless of when cash is received.                                           | Revenues are recorded when cash is **received**.                                                                                                                                         |
| **Expense Recording** | Expenses are recorded when they are **incurred**, regardless of when cash is paid.                                             | Expenses are recorded when cash is **paid**.                                                                                                                                             |
| **"Time-Period Problem"** | Does not have this problem; activities and their economic impact are recognized in the correct period.                         | Has a "time-period problem" because cash flows may not occur in the same period as the economic activity that produces them (e.g., credit transactions).                                  |
| **GAAP & IFRS**       | **Required** by Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) and International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) and **ALWAYS** used. | **NOT accepted** under GAAP or IFRS and **NEVER** used for external financial reporting.                                                                                                 |
| **Purpose**           | Provides a fairer representation of a company's economic activities by matching revenues with the expenses incurred to generate them. | Provides a less accurate picture of a company's financial performance over a period, as it only records transactions when cash changes hands, potentially distorting the timing of economic events. |

[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-3]

### The Revenue Recognition Principle

Revenues should only be recognized when four criteria are met [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-4]:
1.  **Goods have been delivered, or services rendered.**
2.  **Seller's price to buyer is fixed or determinable.**
3.  **Persuasive evidence of a payment arrangement exists.**
4.  **Payment is realized or realizable** (i.e., collectability is reasonably assured).

These conditions are usually met by the time goods/services are delivered, regardless of when cash is received [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-4].

#### Example: Unearned (Deferred) Revenue

**Unearned revenue** occurs when cash is received *before* revenue is earned [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-4].

**Scenario:** McKenz Consulting received $100,000 cash in advance from Tweet Co. in March for consulting services to be delivered in June [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-4].

*   **When should McKenz Consulting recognize revenue?** June.
*   **Journal entries:**
    *   **March:** Cash is received, but services are not yet rendered.
        *   Debit: Cash (Asset) $100,000
        *   Credit: Unearned Consulting Revenue (Liability) $100,000
    *   **June:** Services are delivered, revenue is earned.
        *   Debit: Unearned Consulting Revenue (Liability) $100,000 (to reduce the liability)
        *   Credit: Consulting Revenue (Shareholder's Equity) $100,000 (to recognize revenue)
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-4]

**Another Example: Cruise Ship Construction**
Assume an advance payment of $40 million is received in Year 1 to build a cruise ship over four years, with 25% completed each year [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-4].

*   **Cash-basis:** All $40 million cash receipt is recognized as revenue at the beginning of Year 1. No more revenue records after Year 1. This distorts the revenue recognition.
*   **Accrual-basis:**
    *   At the beginning of Year 1, recognize the $40 million cash receipt as unearned revenue (liability account).
    *   At the end of each year, recognize the portion earned as revenue: $40 million / 4 years = $10 million per year.
    *   This results in a smoother revenue stream and a fairer representation of economic activities [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-4].

*   **Initial Entry (Jan 1, 2020):**
    *   Debit: Cash (A) $40,000,000
    *   Credit: Unearned Revenue (L) $40,000,000
*   **Adjustment Entry (Dec 31, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023):**
    *   Debit: Unearned Revenue (L) $10,000,000
    *   Credit: Sales Revenue (SE) $10,000,000
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-6]

#### Example: Accrued Revenue (Unrecorded Receivables)

**Accrued revenue** occurs when revenue is earned *before* cash is received (i.e., unrecorded receivables) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-4].

**Scenario:** Long-term contract to build a ship starting Jan 1, 2020, with payment of $40 million in cash upon completion in 2023. Assume 25% completion each year [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-4].

*   **Cash-basis:** No revenue recorded for accounting years 1, 2, 3 (unrecorded receivables). Record all $40 million revenue at the end of period 4 when cash is received.
*   **Accrual-basis:**
    *   At the end of each year for years 1, 2, and 3, recognize the portion of revenue earned ($10 million) that has not been received as accounts receivable (asset).
    *   At the end of year 4, recognize the remaining earned revenue, receive cash, and reduce accounts receivable.
    *   This provides a smoother recognition of revenue [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-4].

*   **Initial Entry (Jan 1, 2020):** No entry as no cash transaction occurs yet.
*   **Adjustment Entry (Dec 31, 2020, 2021, 2022):**
    *   Debit: Accounts Receivable (A) $10,000,000
    *   Credit: Sales Revenue (SE) $10,000,000
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-6]
*   **Final Payment Entry (Dec 31, 2023):**
    *   Debit: Cash (A) $40,000,000
    *   Credit: Sales Revenue (SE) $10,000,000 (for current year's earned revenue)
    *   Credit: Accounts Receivable (A) $30,000,000 (for previous years' receivables)
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-6]

### The Matching Principle

Expenses should be **matched** with the revenues they generate [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-4]. If direct matching is not possible, expenses are recognized in the period they occur. Expenses are recognized in the period over which they generate economic benefits, regardless of when cash is paid [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-4].

#### Example: Prepaid (Deferred) Expense

**Prepaid expense** occurs when cash is paid *before* the expense is incurred [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-4].

**Scenario 1: Inventory Purchase and Sale**
BigMart purchased inventory costing $9,000 in cash in January from Johnson Co. BigMart sold the entire inventory for $12,000 cash in May [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-4].

*   **When should BigMart recognize the cost (expense)?** May.
*   **Journal entries:**
    *   **January:** Purchase inventory.
        *   Debit: Inventory (Asset) $9,000
        *   Credit: Cash (Asset) $9,000
    *   **May:** Sell inventory and recognize cost of goods sold.
        *   Debit: Cash (Asset) $12,000
        *   Credit: Sales Revenue (Shareholder's Equity) $12,000
        *   Debit: Cost of Goods Sold (Shareholder's Equity) $9,000
        *   Credit: Inventory (Asset) $9,000
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-4]

**Scenario 2: Rental Fee**
Paid 4 years rental fee of $4 million in advance at the beginning of Year 1. Assume each year's rental is $1 million [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-4].

*   **Cash-basis:** All $4 million is recognized as rental expense at the beginning of Year 1. No more expense records for years 2, 3, 4. This is a poor representation.
*   **Accrual-basis:**
    *   At the beginning of Year 1, recognize the $4 million cash payment as Prepaid Rent (asset).
    *   At the end of each year, recognize the portion used for that year ($1 million) as rental expense.
    *   This results in a fairer representation of expense over the four years [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-4].

*   **Initial Entry (Jan 1, 2020):**
    *   Debit: Prepaid Rent (A) $4,000,000
    *   Credit: Cash (A) $4,000,000
*   **Adjustment Entry (Dec 31, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023):**
    *   Debit: Rent Expense (SE) $1,000,000
    *   Credit: Prepaid Rent (A) $1,000,000
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-6]

**Other Common Types of Prepaid Expenses:**
*   Prepaid Insurance
*   Supplies
*   Prepaid Wages (Salaries)
*   Prepaid Taxes, etc.
These are accounted for similarly to prepaid rent [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-6].

**Special Case: Prepaid Expenses Used Up Within a Single Period**
Some prepaid expenses are both paid for and fully used up within a single period. For example, monthly rent paid on the first day of the month that fully expires by the end of the month. In these cases, the cash paid can be recorded directly with a debit to the expense account instead of a prepaid asset account, and **no adjustment is needed** at the end of the period [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-6].

#### Depreciation for Property, Plant & Equipment (PPE)

**Depreciation** is the allocation of an asset's cost over its estimated useful life [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-6]. The most common method is straight-line depreciation, which allocates the asset's cost (less any residual value) equally over its useful life.

**Scenario:** Buying a truck in Jan 2020 for $200,000. The truck's useful life is 5 years, and residual value is zero [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-6].

*   **Annual Depreciation Expense:** ($200,000 - $0) / 5 years = $40,000 per year.

*   **Initial Entry (Jan 1, 2020):**
    *   Debit: PPE – Truck (A) $200,000
    *   Credit: Cash (A) $200,000
*   **Adjustment Entry (Dec 31, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024):**
    *   When recognizing depreciation expense, the PPE asset account is **not subtracted** directly. Instead, a **Contra-Asset** account called **Accumulated Depreciation** is used. This account is directly linked to the PPE account but has an opposite balance (credit balance).
    *   Debit: Depreciation Expense (Exp) $40,000
    *   Credit: Accumulated Depreciation (XA) $40,000

On the Statement of Financial Position, PPE is reported net of accumulated depreciation. More details on PPE will be covered in [[lecture-09:Lecture 09: Property, Plant & Equipment (PPE) & Intangible Assets]] [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-6].

#### Example: Accrued Expense (Unrecorded Liabilities)

**Accrued expense** occurs when expense is incurred *before* cash is paid (i.e., unrecorded liabilities) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-6].

**Scenario:** Borrow $40 million from a bank in Jan 2020 for a 4-year term. Annual interest rate is 10% (non-compounded). Interest and principal are both paid at the end of the 4th year. We focus on the interest portion, ignoring principal payment [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-6].

*   **Annual Interest Expense:** 10% x $40 million = $4 million per year.
*   **Cash-basis:** Only at Year 4, record Interest Expense of $16 million (10% x $40 million x 4 years) when interest is paid in cash. No record of expense in Year 1, 2, 3 (unrecorded liabilities).
*   **Accrual-basis:**
    *   At the end of each year for years 1, 2, 3, recognize the interest expense portion ($4 million) that is due but not yet paid as interest payable (liability).
    *   At the end of Year 4, recognize the interest expense for that year, pay all interest in cash, and reduce interest payable.
    *   Accrual basis results in a fairer representation of expense over the four years [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-6].

*   **Adjustment Entry (Dec 31, 2020, 2021, 2022):**
    *   Debit: Interest Expense (SE) $4,000,000
    *   Credit: Interest Payable (L) $4,000,000
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-6]
*   **Final Payment Entry (Dec 31, 2023):**
    *   Debit: Interest Expense (SE) $4,000,000 (for current year's interest)
    *   Debit: Interest Payable (L) $12,000,000 (for previous years' accrued interest)
    *   Credit: Cash (A) $16,000,000
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-6]

### Quick Chart of Journal Entries & Financial Statement Effect for Adjustments

Adjusting entries are typically called "AJEs" for short. They are made at the end of an accounting period to bring asset or liability account balances to their proper amounts and usually do not involve cash [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-7]. Adjusting entries always involve one balance sheet account and one income statement account.

#### Adjustments to Revenues

| During the Period       | Cash received before revenue earned                                                                                   |
| :---------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Journal Entry**       | Debit: Cash (A) / Credit: Unearned Revenue (L)                                                                        |
| **End-of-Period Adjustments** | Company has earned revenue in the period (Adjusting Entry)                                                              |
| **Journal Entry**       | Debit: Unearned Revenue (L) / Credit: Sales Revenue (Rev)                                                             |
| **Next Period**         | Cash received after revenue earned                                                                                      |
| **Journal Entry**       | None (or normal journal entry for sales on credit, if applicable, not an adjusting entry at period end)                 |
| **Effect on Financial Statements (Prior to Adjustments)** | **Balance Sheet:** Liability overstated, Equity understated <br/> **Income Statement:** Revenue understated |

| During the Period       | Accrued Revenues (Cash received after revenue earned)                                                                 |
| :---------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Journal Entry**       | None*                                                                                                                 |
| **End-of-Period Adjustments** | Company has earned revenue in the period (Adjusting Entry)                                                              |
| **Journal Entry**       | Debit: Receivables (A) / Credit: Sales Revenue (SE)                                                                   |
| **Next Period**         | Cash received after revenue earned                                                                                      |
| **Journal Entry**       | Debit: Cash (A) / Credit: Receivables (A)                                                                             |
| **Effect on Financial Statements (Prior to Adjustments)** | **Balance Sheet:** Asset understated, Equity understated <br/> **Income Statement:** Revenue understated |

\*Note that sales on credit that happens during the period is typically entered as a normal journal entry during the period as the sales occur; there is NO need to wait till the end of the period to record it as an adjusting entry [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-7].

#### Adjustments to Expenses

| During the Period       | Cash paid before expense incurred (Prepaid Expenses)                                                                  |
| :---------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Journal Entry**       | Debit: Prepaids (A) / Credit: Cash (A)                                                                                |
| **End-of-Period Adjustments** | Company must recognize expense (Adjusting Entry)                                                                        |
| **Journal Entry**       | Debit: Expense (Exp) / Credit: Prepaids (A)                                                                           |
| **Next Period**         | Cash paid after expense incurred                                                                                      |
| **Journal Entry**       | None                                                                                                                  |
| **Effect on Financial Statements (Prior to Adjustments)** | **Balance Sheet:** Asset overstated, Equity overstated <br/> **Income Statement:** Expense understated |

| During the Period       | Cash paid after expense incurred (Accrued Expenses)                                                                   |
| :---------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Journal Entry**       | None*                                                                                                                 |
| **End-of-Period Adjustments** | Company must recognize expense (Adjusting Entry)                                                                        |
| **Journal Entry**       | Debit: Expense (Exp) / Credit: Payables (L)                                                                           |
| **Next Period**         | Cash paid after expense incurred                                                                                      |
| **Journal Entry**       | Debit: Payables (L) / Credit: Cash (A)                                                                                |
| **Effect on Financial Statements (Prior to Adjustments)** | **Balance Sheet:** Liability understated, Equity overstated <br/> **Income Statement:** Expense understated |

\*Note that incurring an expense on credit during the period can also be entered as a normal journal entry [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-7].

### Financial Statement Analysis (FSA) from Lecture 02

Financial ratios are often used to aid in decision making [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-1]. General areas of financial statement analysis include [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-1, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-5]:
1.  **Liquidity and efficiency**: Ability to meet short-term obligations and efficiently generate revenues.
2.  **Solvency**: Ability to meet long-term obligations and generate future revenues.
3.  **Profitability**: Ability to generate attractive and sufficient financial rewards for investors.
4.  **Cash Flow**: Ability to manage cash inflow and outflow.
5.  **Market prospects**: Ability to generate positive market expectations.
(Further financial ratios will be covered in [[module-hub:ACC1701X (hub)]] Chapter 15) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-5].

#### Return on Assets (ROA)

**Return on Assets (ROA)** is a profitability ratio that measures how much return (net profit) a company earns from its assets [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-1, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-5].
*   **Formula**: ROA = Net Profit / Average Total Assets
*   Also known as "Return on Investment."
*   **Uses**: Useful for evaluating management, analyzing profits, forecasting, and benchmarking against competitors or prior years' performance.
*   A **higher ratio is preferred**, indicating a higher return [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-5].

**Example: ComfortDelGro vs. Uber ROA**
| Company       | Year | Net Profit ($mil) | Average Total Assets ($mil) | ROA      |
| :------------ | :--- | :---------------- | :-------------------------- | :------- |
| ComfortDelGro | 2021 | 160               | 4,957                       | 0.0323   |
| ComfortDelGro | 2020 | 92                | 5,169                       | 0.0177   |
| Uber          | 2021 | (-8,506)          | 36,013                      | -0.2362  |
| Uber          | 2020 | (-6,768)          | 32,507                      | -0.2082  |
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-5]

ComfortDelGro's ROA was positive (1.7% in 2020 and 3.2% in 2021), while Uber was unprofitable, showing a negative ROA due to significant losses [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-5].

#### Debt Ratio

**Debt Ratio** is a solvency ratio that measures the proportion of total liabilities a company has relative to its total assets [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-1, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-5].
*   **Formula**: Debt Ratio = Total Liabilities / Total Assets
*   **Uses**: Useful for evaluating the level of debt risk.
*   Companies finance assets through either liabilities or equity. A company using a lot of liabilities is said to have **high financial leverage** (highly leveraged).
*   A **higher ratio indicates higher leverage and higher risk** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-5].

**Example: ComfortDelGro vs. Uber Debt Ratio**
| Company       | Year | Total Liabilities ($mil) | Total Assets ($mil) | Debt Ratio |
| :------------ | :--- | :----------------------- | :------------------ | :--------- |
| ComfortDelGro | 2021 | 1,819                    | 4,955               | 0.3670     |
| ComfortDelGro | 2020 | 1,890                    | 4,959               | 0.3811     |
| ComfortDelGro | 2019 | 2,370                    | 5,379               | 0.4406     |
| Uber          | 2021 | 23,425                   | 38,774              | 0.6041     |
| Uber          | 2020 | 19,498                   | 33,252              | 0.5864     |
| Uber          | 2019 | 16,578                   | 31,761              | 0.5220     |
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-5]

Uber's debt ratio is significantly higher than ComfortDelGro's, indicating Uber is more highly leveraged and faces a higher level of debt risk. Uber's debt ratio also constantly increased from 2019 to 2021 [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-5].

### Take Away for Lecture 03

*   **Periodic accounting** provides timely information, and **accrual-based accounting** ensures a better matching of revenues and expenses [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-8].
*   **Adjustments** are essential at the end of an accounting period to align revenues and expenses with the correct period [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-8].
*   **Adjustments to Revenues**:
    *   **Unearned Revenues**: Cash is received *before* revenue is earned. When revenue is earned, the liability is reduced, and revenue is recognized.
    *   **Accrued Revenues**: Cash is received *after* revenue is earned. When revenue is earned, an asset (receivable) is recorded to show future receipts.
*   **Adjustments to Expenses**:
    *   **Prepaid Expenses**: Expense is incurred *after* cash is paid. When expense is incurred, the prepaid asset is reduced.
    *   **Accrued Expenses**: Expense is incurred *before* cash is paid. When expense is incurred, a liability (payable) is recorded to reflect the future obligation.
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-03-slides-adjusting-accounts-pdf#section-8]

**Summary of Adjusting Journal Entries (AJEs)**:

| Type of Adjustment      | Debit           | Credit          |
| :---------------------- | :-------------- | :-------------- |
| **Unearned Revenues**   | Liability       | Revenue         |
| **Accrued Revenues**    | Asset (Receivable) | Revenue         |
| **Prepaid Expenses**    | Expense         | Asset (Prepaid) |
| **Accrued Expenses**    | Expense         | Liability       |
