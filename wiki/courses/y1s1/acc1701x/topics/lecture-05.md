### Potential Problems in Financial Statements

Financial statements are susceptible to various issues that can compromise their integrity [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-2]:

1.  **Errors**: These are unintentional mistakes that, once detected, should be corrected. Examples include [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-2]:
    *   Not recording a transaction (e.g., a lost supplier invoice).
    *   Entering an incorrect amount or information (e.g., an invoice for $500 entered as $5,000).
    *   Debiting or crediting the wrong accounts.
2.  **Disagreements on Judgement**: Accounting often involves estimations and judgments, leading different parties to potentially arrive at different conclusions based on the same facts. An example is determining the amount of revenue "earned" for projects spanning multiple periods [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-2].
3.  **Frauds**: These are intentional errors made to manipulate financial statements for personal gain or to misrepresent financial results [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-2].

#### Effects of Errors on Financial Statements: Examples

Understanding how errors impact financial statements is crucial. The following examples illustrate common mistakes and their effects:

**Example 1: Rent Expense Misclassification (Company PopIt)**
Company PopIt paid its 2022 rent in advance in 2021 but mistakenly recorded the entire amount as Rent Expense in 2021 [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-6].

*   **Incorrect Entry (2021)**: Dr Rent Expense, Cr Cash [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-1].
*   **Correct Entry (2021)**: Dr Prepaid Rent, Cr Cash [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-1].
*   **Effects on 2021 Financial Statements** [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-1]:
    *   **Net Income (NI)**: Overstated expense leads to **understated NI**.
    *   **Statement of Financial Position (SFP)**: Understated NI results in **understated Equity**; the missing prepaid rent leads to **understated Assets**.
*   **Correct Entry (2022)**: Dr Rent Expense, Cr Prepaid Rent [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-1].
*   **Effects on 2022 Financial Statements (if not corrected in 2021)** [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-1]:
    *   **Net Income (NI)**: Missing expense leads to **overstated NI**. (The 2021 NI understatement is effectively offset by a 2022 NI overstatement, correcting the equity balance over two years).
    *   **Statement of Financial Position (SFP)**: The prepaid asset is no longer missing, and the equity balance is correctly stated after two years [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-1].

**Example 2: Double Payment of Accounts Payable (Company PopIt)**
Company PopIt mistakenly recorded the payment of an accounts payable twice [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-7].

*   **Incorrect Entry**: Dr Accounts Payable, Cr Cash (recorded twice) [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-2].
*   **Effects on Financial Statements** [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-2]:
    *   **Statement of Financial Position (SFP)**: Accounts Payable is reduced too much, leading to **understated Liabilities**. Cash is also reduced too much, leading to **understated Assets**.
    *   **Net Income (NI)**: No effect, as no revenue or expense accounts are impacted [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-2].

**Example 3: PPE Misclassified as Expense (Company PopIt)**
Company PopIt mistakenly recorded the purchase of $1,600 PPE for cash by debiting Miscellaneous Expense and crediting Cash [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-7].

*   **Incorrect Entry**: Dr Misc. Expense $1,600, Cr Cash $1,600 [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-3].
*   **Effects on Financial Statements** [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-3]:
    *   **Net Income (NI)**: An extra expense was recorded, resulting in **understated NI**.
    *   **Statement of Financial Position (SFP)**: Understated NI leads to **understated Equity**. The PPE asset is missing, resulting in **understated Assets**.
*   **Correcting Journal Entry**: Cr Misc. Expense $1,600, Dr PPE $1,600 [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-3].

**In-Class Activity: Blossom Co. - Correcting Errors**
The following are examples of errors and their effects on Blossom Co.'s financial statements if not corrected [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-22]:

*   **a) Unrecorded Accrued Salaries**: Salaries earned in December (not paid until January) were not adjusted.
    *   **Effect if not corrected**: Expenses understated, Net Income overstated; Liabilities understated, Equity overstated [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-4].
    *   **Correcting Entry**: Dr Salaries Expense $15,000, Cr Salaries Payable $15,000 [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-4].
*   **b) Prepaid Insurance Expensed Immediately**: A 12-month insurance policy paid on Nov 1 was fully debited to insurance expense on Nov 1.
    *   **Effect if not corrected**: Expenses overstated, Net Income understated; Assets understated, Equity understated [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-5].
    *   **Correcting Entry**: Dr Prepaid Insurance $10,000, Cr Insurance Expense $10,000 (to recognize the unexpired portion) [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-5].
*   **c) Unearned Revenue Recorded as Service Revenue**: A $5,000 prepayment for services (to be performed in January) was debited to Cash and credited to Service Revenue on Dec 15.
    *   **Effect if not corrected**: Revenue overstated, Net Income overstated; Liabilities understated, Equity overstated [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-6].
    *   **Correcting Entry**: Dr Service Revenue $5,000, Cr Unearned Revenue $5,000 [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-6].
*   **d) Accounts Receivable Receipt Recorded Twice**: A $15,000 receipt for accounts receivable was recorded twice.
    *   **Effect if not corrected**: No effect on Net Income (assuming no bad debt expense calculation impact); Cash (assets) overstated by $15,000, Accounts Receivable (assets) understated by $15,000, resulting in no overall SFP effect [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-7].
    *   **Correcting Entry**: Dr Cash $15,000 (to remove the extra debit), Cr Accounts Receivable $15,000 (to reverse the extra credit) [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-7].
*   **e) Loan Recorded at Wrong Amount**: A $20,000 loan was inadvertently recorded as $200,000.
    *   **Effect if not corrected**: No effect on Net Income (assuming no interest expense impact); Assets overstated by $180,000, Liabilities overstated by $180,000 [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-8].
    *   **Correcting Entry**: Dr Notes Payable $180,000, Cr Cash $180,000 (to correct the excess recording) [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-8].
*   **f) Depreciation Recorded by Crediting Cash**: Depreciation expense of $35,000 was recorded by crediting cash.
    *   **Effect if not corrected**: No effect on Net Income; Cash (asset) understated by $35,000, Accumulated Depreciation (contra-asset) understated by $35,000, leading to an overstatement of the net PPE account [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-9].
    *   **Correcting Entry**: Dr Cash $35,000, Cr Accumulated Depreciation $35,000 [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-9].
*   **g) Inventory Recorded as Supplies**: The acquisition of inventory for $40,000 was inadvertently recorded to the supplies account.
    *   **Effect if not corrected**: No effect on Net Income (assuming inventory was not sold); Inventory (asset) understated by $40,000, Supplies (asset) overstated by $40,000, leading to no overall SFP effect [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-10].
    *   **Correcting Entry**: Dr Inventory $40,000, Cr Supplies $40,000 [source: acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-05-supplement-pdf#section-10].

### What is Fraud?

Fraud is generally defined as an attempt to deceive others for personal gain [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-8]. It typically manifests in three main categories:

1.  **Corruption**: Misusing one's position for inappropriate personal gain [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-8].
2.  **Asset Misappropriation**: Theft or embezzlement of company resources [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-8].
3.  **Financial Statement Fraud**: Misreporting amounts in the financial statements to portray more favorable financial results [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-8].

#### The Fraud Triangle

The Fraud Triangle explains the three conditions typically present when fraud occurs [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-9]:

*   **Opportunity**: The means to commit fraud, often facilitated by weak internal controls [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-9].
*   **Rationalization**: Justification for the fraudulent action, where a personal feeling of entitlement or the belief that the action is unavoidable/necessary outweighs moral principles [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-9].
*   **Pressure**: Financial, personal, or societal goals/expectations that create an incentive to commit fraud (e.g., meeting loan covenants, maintaining stock price, attracting investors) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-9].

### Internal Control System

An internal control system comprises policies and procedures designed to [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-10]:

*   Ensure reliable and accurate financial records.
*   Properly account for and safeguard assets.
*   Promote efficient operations.
*   Ensure adherence to company policies.
*   Urge compliance with laws and regulations.

#### Internal Control Structure

The Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission (COSO) framework identifies five basic categories for an effective internal control structure [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-11]:

1.  **Control Environment**: Reflects the corporate culture and top management's attitude towards internal control. A clear organizational structure defines lines of authority and responsibility [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-11].
2.  **Monitoring**: Involves independent oversight of management, typically through the Board of Directors (BOD) and internal and external auditors [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-11].
3.  **Risk Assessment**: This is usually covered in auditing courses and involves identifying and analyzing risks to achieving objectives [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-11].
4.  **Information and Communication**: Also typically covered in auditing courses, this ensures relevant information is identified, captured, and communicated in a timely manner [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-11].
5.  **Control Activities**: These are the specific policies and procedures implemented by management to achieve their objectives [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-11].

#### Principles of Internal Control (Control Activities)

Control activities can be broadly categorized into preventive and detective controls:

**Preventive Controls** aim to stop errors or fraud from occurring in the first place [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-12]:

1.  **Establish Responsibilities and Segregate Duties**:
    *   No single person or department should be responsible for all conflicting parts of a process.
    *   For example, an employee in charge of purchasing from a supplier should not also approve payments [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-12].
2.  **Proper Procedures for Authorization**:
    *   Implement different levels of authority and authorization limits for specific transactions [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-12].
3.  **Control Assets and Records**:
    *   Separate recordkeeping from the custody of assets. A person with control or access to an asset should not also keep its accounting records (e.g., a warehouse manager controlling inventory should not have access to inventory accounting records) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-12].

**Detective Controls** aim to uncover errors or fraud that have already occurred [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-13]:

1.  **Maintain Adequate Records**:
    *   Good recordkeeping protects assets and ensures compliance with prescribed procedures (e.g., using prenumbered checks for supplier payments, digitally documenting electronic fund transfers) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-13].
2.  **Perform Regular and Independent Reviews**:
    *   Check others' work (e.g., supervisors reviewing subordinates' work).
    *   Conduct frequent reviews of procedures to evaluate effectiveness and promote adherence.
    *   Internal auditors, not directly involved in the audited activities, provide impartial perspectives.
    *   External auditors, independent of the company, provide assurance of financial statements [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-13].

#### Limitations of Internal Control

Internal controls, while vital, cannot completely prevent and detect all errors and fraud due to [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-14]:

*   **Human Error**: Negligence, fatigue, misjudgment, confusion, or carelessness can lead to mistakes [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-14].
*   **Human Fraud**: Intentional acts to defeat internal control or collusion for personal gain [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-14].
*   **Cost-Benefit Principle**: Companies can only implement internal controls if the benefits outweigh the costs. For example, extreme measures to prevent shoplifting might drive away customers, leading to greater losses than the shoplifting itself [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-14].

### Role of Auditors

Auditors play a critical role in ensuring financial statement integrity by providing assurance to management and stockholders.

**Internal Auditors** are an independent group of experts within a company, focusing on controls, accounting, and operations. Their functions include [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-15]:

*   Monitoring operating results and financial records.
*   Evaluating internal controls.
*   Assisting in increasing operational efficiency and effectiveness.
*   Ensuring compliance with laws and regulations.
*   Detecting fraud.

**External Auditors** are independent certified public accountants (CPAs) who examine an organization's financial statements. Their primary role is to determine if the statements are prepared and presented in accordance with prevailing accounting standards (e.g., IFRS, GAAP) and are free from material misstatement [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-16].

*   They issue opinions on the reliability of an organization's internal controls.
*   They disclose Key Audit Matters (KAMs) in the audit report, which highlight areas of significant management judgment [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-16].
*   It is crucial to note that auditors provide **reasonable assurance** that financial statements are "presented fairly," not a guarantee that they are "correct" [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-16].

### Earnings Quality and Earnings Management

The objective of financial statements is to provide useful, high-quality information to users. High-quality information must be both relevant and reliable (free from error and fair representation) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-19].

**Earnings Management** (or "cooking the books") occurs when statements do not reflect what truly happened, but rather what management wants users to see. This involves using accounting gimmicks to make financial results appear better than they are [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-19].

#### Why Companies "Cook" the Books (Incentives)

Firms and individuals have various incentives to manage reported financial statements and earnings [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-20]:

*   **To meet internal targets**: Such as management bonus thresholds.
*   **To meet external expectations**: From investors, customers, suppliers, and financial analysts. Missing an analyst's earnings forecast can lead to a drop in share price, so companies might prefer to report a small profit over a small loss.
*   **To smooth income**: To appear less risky or cover up mistakes. A smooth trend of earnings is often perceived more favorably than volatile earnings.
*   **To "window dress"**: To present a good financial appearance for events like an Initial Public Offering (IPO) or securing a bank loan.

As a financial statement user, it is important to critically scrutinize the estimates and assumptions made by firms, especially when companies are under pressure to manage earnings [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-21].

#### How Companies "Cook" the Books

Common ways companies manipulate their books include [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-25]:

*   **Manipulating Revenues**: Improper recognition, fake/fictitious revenues, transactions with related parties, or "channel stuffing" (shipping excess products to distributors to boost sales).
*   **Manipulating Estimates**: Using inappropriate methodologies for estimates like depreciation or write-offs.
*   **Manipulating Expenses**: Improper capitalization of expenses (treating an expense as an asset to delay recognition).
*   **Manipulating Cash Flow**: Altering the classification of cash flows.
*   **Manipulating Weak Internal Controls**: Exploiting weaknesses in the control system.

**Cases of Revenue Recognition Gimmicks (Examples)** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-26]:

*   **Xerox (2002)**: Fined $10 million by the SEC for improperly classifying short-term equipment rentals as long-term leases, accelerating revenue recognition. They also stored revenue off-balance sheet in a "cookie jar" to strategically boost earnings.
*   **Nortel Networks (2007)**: Fined $35 million by the SEC for fraudulently accelerating revenue in 2000 to meet targets and using a "cookie jar" of reserves to turn a 2003 loss into profit, enabling large bonuses.
*   **GROUPON (2011)**: The SEC forced Groupon to restate earnings for 2009-2011 before its IPO. Groupon had recognized the entire price of a deal as revenue, rather than just its commission, inflating sales revenue.

### Cash Flow Hocus Pocus: Cases of Cash Flow Manipulation

While cash might seem tangible and hard to manipulate, companies can still engage in "cash flow hocus pocus" [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-27].

*   **CASE 1: Netflix (Misclassifying Inventory Purchases)**:
    *   When Netflix started, its core business was renting DVDs. For years, it treated DVD purchases (essential inventory) as an **investing outflow** instead of an **operating outflow** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-27].
    *   This was curious because most new DVDs were amortized over one year. By classifying them as investing activities, Netflix significantly boosted its operating cash flows, making them appear stronger [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-27].
    *   **Red Flag**: Always question large investing outflows that appear to be part of a company's normal cost of operations [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-27].

*   **CASE 2: Oxford Industries (Securitizations)**:
    *   Oxford Industries doubled its Cash Flow from Operations (CFO) in 2001, largely due to a massive decline in Accounts Receivable [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-28].
    *   This was not because customers suddenly paid up, but because Oxford **sold $80.5 million of its receivables** (known as "securitization") to third parties [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-28].
    *   **Red Flag**: Be cautious when companies initiate or increase securitization programs, as this can create a misleading impression of improved CFO [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-28].

### Accounting Scandals (Not Examinable)

The following accounting scandals serve as examples of severe financial statement fraud and their consequences [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-23, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-24]:

*   **Enron (2001)**:
    *   A Houston-based energy giant, once the 7th largest U.S. company with $100 billion in revenues [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-29].
    *   Investigated by the SEC in 2001, it filed for bankruptcy with $74 billion in losses, wiping out employee retirement accounts [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-29].
    *   **Schemes**: Inflated income and profit through long-term energy contracts, hid losses and debts in over 500 off-balance sheet Special Purpose Entities (SPEs), inflated stock prices, funneled funds to personal parties, misrepresented financial performance, and reduced tax payments [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-30, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-31].
    *   **Major Perpetrators**: Ken Lay (Chairman/CEO), Jeff Skilling (CEO), Andrew Fastow (CFO). The accounting firm Arthur Andersen collapsed due to its involvement [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-29].

*   **Satyam (2009)**:
    *   An Indian IT services company, once a pioneering tech firm [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-32].
    *   In December 2008, its founder-chairman Raju confessed that the company's accounts had been falsified for many years [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-32].
    *   **Scam Details**: Inflated revenues via false sales invoices, forged bank documents to show non-existent cash deposits, funneled funds to family-controlled firms that bought massive land and property, used 356 investment companies to divert funds, and created 13,000 fake non-existent employees to embezzle funds [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-33].
    *   **Major Perpetrators**: B. Ramalinga Raju (Founder/Chairman), B. Rama Raju (Brother/Managing Dir.), V Srinivas (CFO), and PwC (Auditor) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-32].

*   **Wirecard (2020)**:
    *   A German payments processing fintech, once hailed as the "German Paypal," with a market cap of $24 billion at its peak [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-34].
    *   Declared insolvency in June 2020, owing creditors €3.5 billion, after its stock price plunged from €100 to €2 in days [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-34].
    *   **Accounting Fraud**: Fraudulently inflated sales and profits, with €1.9 billion cash "missing" (one-quarter of its balance sheet) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-35].
    *   Wirecard lied to its auditors (EY) about large sums of money held in a Singaporean bank (OCBC). EY failed to verify the funds, relying instead on forged documents. Wirecard even tried to acquire Deutsche Bank and hired actors in the Philippines to impersonate bank officials to "convince" auditors [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-35].
    *   In April 2020, KPMG could not verify the funds. In June 2020, EY refused to sign off on the annual audit, stating the €1.9 billion cash was "spurious," leading to Wirecard's collapse [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-35].
    *   **Perpetrators**: CEO Marcus Braum was arrested, while CFO Jan Marsalek "vanished" and is now one of the most wanted men globally [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-05-slides-fs-integrity-pdf#section-34].

### Key Takeaways for Lecture 05

*   Financial statements can be affected by unintentional errors, disagreements in judgments and estimates, and intentional fraud.
*   Internal controls are crucial policies and procedures designed to safeguard assets and ensure the accuracy of financial records.
*   Both internal and external auditors play a vital role in providing assurance regarding the reliability and fair presentation of financial statements.
*   Earnings management refers to the manipulation of financial results, driven by various incentives to present a more favorable financial picture.
