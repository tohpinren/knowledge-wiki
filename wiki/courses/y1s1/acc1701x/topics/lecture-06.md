# Lecture 06: Receivables

This page consolidates the numbered lecture materials for ACC1701X: Lecture 06, covering Accounts Receivables (AR) and Notes Receivables. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-1, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-2, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-3, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-4, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-5]

**Key Concepts:**
*   **Receivables:** Amounts due from another party. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-7]
*   **Accounts Receivables (Trade Receivables):** Amounts due from customers for credit sales/services in the normal course of business; a liquid asset reported after cash. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-7]
*   **Notes Receivables:** A promissory note to pay a specified amount, usually with interest, on demand or a definite future date. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-7, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-27]
*   **Expected Credit Loss (ECL):** An estimate of uncollectible amounts from receivables, recognized in the same period as the related sales, aligning with the Matching Principle. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-10]
*   **Allowance Method:** An accounting method for credit losses, estimating ECL based on impairment signs, using a contra-asset account called Loss Allowance. This method is accepted under IFRS/GAAP. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-11, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-13]
*   **Accounts Receivables Turnover:** Measures how often receivables are collected and converted to cash, indicating efficiency in credit management. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-34]
*   **Average Collection Period:** Measures the average number of days it takes to collect receivables, reflecting liquidity. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-36]

### Major Activities of a Business

Business activities are categorized into three main types:
1.  **Operating Activities:** Associated with the primary purpose of the business. These include selling products or services, buying inventory for resale, and incurring and paying for necessary expenses in primary operations. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-3]
2.  **Investing Activities:** Involve the purchase of assets (e.g., Property, Plant & Equipment, financial assets) for use in the business. These occur less frequently and typically involve larger amounts. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-3]
3.  **Financing Activities:** Focus on raising money to finance a business, either through equity financing (selling ownership) or debt financing (borrowing from creditors). [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-3]

This module covers these activities as follows:

*   **Investing Activities:** Property, Plant & Equipment (PPE) (Chapter 10) and Investments (not covered in this module).
*   **Financing Activities:** Equity Financing (Chapter 12) and Long-Term Debt Financing (not covered in this module).
*   **Financial Statements:** Statement of Cash Flow (Chapter 14) and Financial Statement Analysis (Chapter 15). [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-4]

### What are Receivables?

A receivable is an amount due from another party [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-7]. There are several types of receivables:

*   **Accounts Receivables (Trade Receivables):**
    *   Amounts due from customers as a result of selling goods or providing services on credit during the normal course of business. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-7]
    *   Considered a fairly liquid asset, typically reported on the Statement of Financial Position (SFP) after cash and current investments. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-7]

*   **Notes Receivables:**
    *   A promissory note is a written promise to pay a specified amount of money, usually with interest, either on demand or at a definite future date. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-7, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-27]
    *   They typically bear interest, involve larger amounts, and have longer credit periods compared to accounts receivables. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-27]
    *   Notes receivables can arise from:
        1.  **Sales:** Similar to Accounts Receivable but for larger sales with longer credit periods. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-27]
        2.  **Extension of AR:** Converting an existing accounts receivable into a note to provide the customer with additional time to pay. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-7, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-27]
        3.  **Lending:** Providing a loan to an external party, such as a supplier or business partner. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-27]

*   **Other Receivables:**
    *   Include interest receivables, rent receivables, and receivables from employees. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-7]

### Reporting Credit Sales Revenue

When a company makes a sale on credit, it records the revenue and creates an accounts receivable. When the customer pays, cash is collected and the accounts receivable is reduced.

1.  **To record credit Sales Revenue:**
    *   Debit: Accounts Receivable – Customer Name (XXX)
    *   Credit: Sales Revenue (XXX)
    *   *(Note: Remember to also recognize the related Cost of Goods Sold (COGS).)* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-8]

2.  **To collect on credit sales:**
    *   Debit: Cash (XXX)
    *   Credit: Accounts Receivable – Customer Name (XXX) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-8]

#### Subsidiary Accounts vs. General Ledger

Companies maintain a separate account for each customer, tracking their purchases, payments, and outstanding amounts. This supplementary record is called the **accounts receivables ledger** (or subsidiary ledger). The total of all individual customer balances in the subsidiary ledger must equal the balance in the single **Accounts Receivable control account** in the General Ledger. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-9]

For example, if Company Alpha owes $700, Company Beta owes $150, and Company Sigma owes $450, their individual balances are recorded in the AR Ledger. The General Ledger's Accounts Receivable account would show a total balance of $1,300 (700 + 150 + 450). [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-9]

### Valuing Receivables: Accounting for Credit Loss

Not all receivables are collectible, meaning a portion of accounts receivable may ultimately not be collected [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-10].

Under **FRS 109 (IFRS 9)**, accounts receivable must be assessed for any possibility of impairment. This requires recognizing an **Expected Credit Loss (ECL)** for uncollectible amounts in the same period that the sales occur, in accordance with the [[lecture-03#The Matching Principle|Matching Principle]]. This ensures that revenues and related expenses (credit losses) are recognized in the same period. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-10]

Variations of the name for ECL include:
*   Bad debt expense
*   Doubtful account expense
*   AR impairment loss
*   Uncollectible account expense

For the purpose of this module, either ECL (Expected Credit Loss) or Bad Debt Expense can be used. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-10]

There are two methods of accounting for credit loss:

1.  **Direct Write-Off Method:**
    *   This method directly writes off a customer's accounts receivable account to Expected Credit Loss (ECL) when it becomes clear that the amount is uncollectible.
    *   **Journal Entry for Write-Off:**
        *   Debit: Expected Credit Loss (XXX)
        *   Credit: Accounts Receivable – Customer Name (XXX)
    *   If an account later becomes collectible, a reversing entry is needed to reinstate the accounts receivable, followed by a second entry to record the cash received.
    *   **Journal Entry for Reinstatement:**
        *   Debit: Accounts Receivable – Customer Name (XXX)
        *   Credit: Expected Credit Loss (XXX)
    *   **Journal Entry for Collection:**
        *   Debit: Cash (XXX)
        *   Credit: Accounts Receivable – Customer Name (XXX)
    *   **This method is generally NOT accepted under IFRS/GAAP** because it violates the matching principle and the principle of conservatism. However, some companies may use it if impairment losses are insignificant. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-11, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-12]

2.  **Allowance Method:**
    *   This method is **accepted under IFRS/GAAP**. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-11]
    *   Companies *estimate* the ECL for receivables that *may* turn bad based on signs of impairment at the end of each period, rather than waiting for them to actually turn bad. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-13]
    *   **Advantages of the Allowance Method:**
        1.  It records ECL in the same period as the related sales, adhering to the Matching Principle. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-13]
        2.  Accounts receivable is reported on the Statement of Financial Position (SFP) at the estimated amount of cash to be collected (i.e., Accounts Receivable balance less Allowance for ECL). This provides a more realistic representation of the company's assets. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-13]

#### Two Main Steps in the Allowance Method

1.  **Estimate and record ECL at the end of the period.**
    *   A **contra-asset account** called **Loss Allowance** (or Allowance for ECL/Allowance for Bad Debts) is used.
    *   This is typically done through an Adjusting Journal Entry (AJE) at the end of the accounting period. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-14]

    *   **Example (Recording ECL):**
        *   At the end of 2022, Takashimaya estimates that 10% of its $500,000 Accounts Receivable may be uncollectible. (10% x $500,000 = $50,000)
        *   **Journal Entry (Dec 2022):**
            *   Debit: Expected Credit Loss ($50,000)
            *   Credit: Loss Allowance ($50,000)
        *   **Impact on Financial Statements:**
            *   **Statement of Financial Position:** Accounts Receivable $500,000, Less Loss Allowance $(50,000), Accounts Receivable (net) $450,000.
            *   **Income Statement:** Expenses: Expected Credit Loss $50,000. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-15]

2.  **Write-off specific customer's accounts receivable once they are determined to be uncollectible.**
    *   The contra-asset account Loss Allowance is reduced during write-offs.
    *   The customer's specific accounts receivable is removed.
    *   Write-offs can be done at any time in other periods. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-14]

    *   **Example (Writing off uncollectible account):**
        *   In February 2023, Soft Towel Store goes bankrupt, and Takashimaya determines its $2,500 accounts receivable will be uncollectible.
        *   **Journal Entry (Feb 2023):**
            *   Debit: Loss Allowance ($2,500)
            *   Credit: Accounts Receivable – Soft Towel (A) ($2,500)
        *   **Impact:**
            *   Writing off uncollectible accounts does **not** affect any income statement accounts because the impairment loss (ECL) was already taken in the period of sale (e.g., 2022).
            *   The write-off also does **not** affect the realizable value of accounts receivable (Accounts Receivable (net) on the SFP).
            *   **Before Write-Off:** Accounts Receivable $500,000, Less Loss Allowance $50,000, Accounts Receivable (net) $450,000.
            *   **After Write-Off:** Accounts Receivable $497,500 (500,000 - 2,500), Less Loss Allowance $47,500 (50,000 - 2,500), Accounts Receivable (net) $450,000. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-16]

#### Estimation of Loss Allowance under FRS 109 (IFRS 9)

With effect from 1st January 2018, under FRS 109 (IFRS 9):
*   The **Expected Credit Loss (ECL) model** is used to estimate impairment of accounts receivable.
*   For short-term accounts receivable, the loss allowance estimate should be equal to the lifetime expected credit losses.
*   An entity may use a simplified **"provision matrix"** for calculating expected losses as a practical expedient for receivables.
    *   A provision matrix may specify provision rates based on the number of days an accounts receivable is past due, with higher rates for longer due accounts (**Aging Analysis**).
    *   Accounts may also be grouped based on reasonable groupings such as geographical regions, product types, or customer ratings. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-17]

**Steps in Estimation of Allowance:**
The general approach to estimating the allowance involves:
1.  **Individual Assessment:** Identify specific accounts with known credit problems.
2.  **Group (Collective) Assessment:** For all other accounts receivable with no known credit problems, use a provision matrix to perform aging analysis:
    *   Classify each receivable by how long it is past due.
    *   Multiply each age group by its estimated uncollectible percentage.
    *   Total the estimated loss allowance for each group.
3.  **Total Allowance:** Combine estimates from individual and group assessment to determine the total allowance required at the reporting date. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-18]

**Example: Fab Co. Estimation of Loss Allowance**

Fab Co. has an accounts receivable balance of $600,000 on December 31, 2022.
*   **Customer – Cool Co.:** $100,000 (Objective evidence of trouble, some amount uncollectible).
*   **All other customers:** $500,000 (No obvious evidence of uncollectibility). [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-19]

**STEP 1 – Individual Assessment:**
*   Fab Co. estimates a 90% chance that the entire $100,000 owed by Cool Co. will be uncollectible.
*   Expected Credit Loss = 90% x $100,000 = $90,000.
*   **Journal Entry:**
    *   Debit: Expected Credit Loss ($90,000)
    *   Credit: Loss Allowance ($90,000) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-20]

**STEP 2 – Group Assessment (Aging Analysis) for remaining $500,000:**
*   Based on FRS 109 (IFRS 9), use a provision matrix to collectively assess the accounts by performing an aging analysis.
*   Categorize all accounts receivables according to their age (how long they are overdue at Dec 31, 2022).
*   Estimate uncollectible amounts for each age category based on historical loss experience and current economic conditions. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-21, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-22]

| Customer        | Not Yet Due | 1-30 Days | 31-60 Days | 61-90 Days | Over 90 Days | Total     |
| :-------------- | :---------- | :-------- | :--------- | :--------- | :----------- | :-------- |
| Company A       | 60,000      | 20,000    | 30,000     | 30,000     | 20,000       | 160,000   |
| Company B       | 130,000     | 35,000    | 40,000     | -          | -            | 205,000   |
| Company C       | -           | -         | 55,000     | 20,000     | 35,000       | 110,000   |
| ...             | :           | :         | :          | :          | :            | :         |
| Company Z       | -           | -         | -          | -          | 25,000       | 25,000    |
| **Total**       | **190,000** | **55,000** | **125,000** | **50,000** | **80,000**    | **500,000** |
| Estimated %     | 1%          | 5%        | 15%        | 25%        | 50%          |           |
| **Estimated Loss** | **1,900**   | **2,750** | **18,750** | **12,500** | **40,000**   | **75,900** |
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-22]

The **target balance** of the Loss Allowance account under Group Assessment should be **$75,900**. This amount is used to adjust the Loss Allowance account. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-22, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-23]

**Calculating ECL for Group Assessment (Adjusting Journal Entry):**
The adjusting entry brings the Loss Allowance account to its target balance, recognizing the necessary ECL. (Remember: Loss Allowance is a contra-asset account and has a normal credit balance).

*   **Scenario 1: No existing balance in Loss Allowance.**
    *   Adjustment needed: $75,900
    *   **Adjusting Journal Entry:**
        *   Debit: Expected Credit Loss ($75,900)
        *   Credit: Loss Allowance ($75,900) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-23]

*   **Scenario 2: Existing normal (credit) balance of $27,000 in Loss Allowance.**
    *   Adjustment needed: $75,900 (target) - $27,000 (existing) = $48,900
    *   **Adjusting Journal Entry:**
        *   Debit: Expected Credit Loss ($48,900)
        *   Credit: Loss Allowance ($48,900) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-24]

*   **Scenario 3: Existing abnormal (debit) balance of $13,000 in Loss Allowance.**
    *   An abnormal debit balance occurs when write-offs exceed the estimated allowances, indicating underestimation.
    *   Adjustment needed: $75,900 (target) + $13,000 (to offset debit) = $88,900
    *   **Adjusting Journal Entry:**
        *   Debit: Expected Credit Loss ($88,900)
        *   Credit: Loss Allowance ($88,900) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-24]

**STEP 3 – Total Loss Allowance and ECL:**
Combine the individual and group assessments to get the total impact on the financial statements.

| SFP Effect                     | Individual Assessment | Group Assessment | Total       | To Statement of Financial Position (SFP) |
| :----------------------------- | :-------------------- | :--------------- | :---------- | :--------------------------------------- |
| Accounts Receivable            | 100,000               | 500,000          | **600,000** |                                          |
| Less Loss Allowance\*          | (90,000)              | (75,900)         | **(165,900)** |                                          |
| **Accounts Receivable (net)**  | **10,000**            | **424,100**      | **434,100** |                                          |

| Income Statement Effect       | Individual Assessment | Group Assessment (Scenario) | Total        | To Income Statement |
| :---------------------------- | :-------------------- | :-------------------------- | :----------- | :------------------ |
| Expected Credit Loss (Scenario 1) | 90,000                | 75,900                      | **165,900**  |                     |
| Expected Credit Loss (Scenario 2) | 90,000                | 48,900                      | **138,900**  |                     |
| Expected Credit Loss (Scenario 3) | 90,000                | 88,900                      | **178,900**  |                     |
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-25]

### Recording Notes Receivable

When Fab Co. receives a 6-month note of $50,000 at a 10% annual interest rate on October 1st, 2022, the journal entries vary based on how the note arises:

1.  **For Sales:**
    *   Debit: Notes Receivable ($50,000)
    *   Credit: Sales Revenue ($50,000) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-28]

2.  **For Conversion from Accounts Receivable:**
    *   Debit: Notes Receivable ($50,000)
    *   Credit: Accounts Receivable ($50,000) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-28]

3.  **For Lending:**
    *   Debit: Notes Receivable ($50,000)
    *   Credit: Cash ($50,000) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-28]

#### Recording Interest for Short-Term Notes

At year-end (December 31st, 2022), an adjusting entry is needed to accrue interest income for the note, even if payment has not been received. The $50,000 note starts on October 1st, 2022, so 3 months of interest (Oct, Nov, Dec) needs to be accrued for 2022.

*   Interest Income = $50,000 x 10% x (3/12) = $1,250
*   **Adjusting Entry at Year-End (Dec 31st, 2022):**
    *   Debit: Interest Receivable ($1,250)
    *   Credit: Interest Income ($1,250)
    *   *(Note: This computation is for short-term notes. Long-term notes involve time value of money, not covered in this module.)* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-29]

#### Recording Collection of Principal + Interest

On March 31st, 2023, Fab Co. collects the note's principal and interest. First, recognize interest income for the remaining 3 months of Jan-Mar 2023.

*   Interest Income for Jan-Mar 2023 = $50,000 x 10% x (3/12) = $1,250
*   **Journal Entry to Record Collection:**
    *   Debit: Cash ($52,500) *(Principal $50,000 + Accrued Interest $1,250 (from 2022) + New Interest Income $1,250 (from 2023))*
    *   Credit: Interest Receivable ($1,250) *(To clear the accrued interest from 2022)*
    *   Credit: Interest Income ($1,250) *(To record interest income for 2023)*
    *   Credit: Note Receivable ($50,000) *(To clear the principal note)* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-30]

### Disposal of Receivables

Companies can convert receivables into cash before they are due through two primary methods:

1.  **Selling Receivables:**
    *   Selling receivables to a finance company or bank.
    *   The buyer takes ownership of the receivables. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-31]

2.  **Pledging Receivables:**
    *   Using receivables as security for a loan.
    *   The company (borrower of the loan) still retains ownership of the receivables. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-31]

### Financial Analysis: Assessing Efficiency and Liquidity

Two key ratios are used to assess how efficiently a company manages its receivables and its liquidity:

1.  **Accounts Receivables Turnover (AR Turnover):**
    *   **Formula:** Accounts Receivables Turnover = Net Sales / Average Accounts Receivable [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-34]
    *   **Purpose:** Measures how often receivables are received and collected, indicating how many times a year a company converts its average accounts receivables into cash. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-34]
    *   **Usefulness:** Assesses how efficient management has been in granting credit to produce revenue. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-34]
    *   **Average Accounts Receivable (AR):** (Beginning AR + Ending AR) / 2 [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-34]

2.  **Average Collection Period:**
    *   **Formula:** Average Collection Period = 365 / Accounts Receivable Turnover [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-36]
    *   **Purpose:** Measures the average number of days it takes the company to collect on its accounts receivables and convert them to cash. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-36]
    *   **Usefulness:** Useful for assessing a company's liquidity (how easily an asset can be converted to cash) and the efficiency of collecting receivables. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-36]
    *   Also known as "Days to Collect AR." [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-36]
    *   **Interpretation:** A shorter collection period is generally preferred, indicating faster conversion to cash. [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-06-slides-receivables-pdf#section-36]
