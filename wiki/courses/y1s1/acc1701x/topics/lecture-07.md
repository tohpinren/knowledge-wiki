# Lecture 07: Cash & Current Liabilities

This page covers the core concepts of Cash (Chapter 6) and Current Liabilities (Chapter 9, LO3) as presented in Lecture 07 of ACC1701X. It also introduces key financial analysis ratios related to liquidity.

### Overview

This lecture aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of cash management, internal controls, buying and selling transactions, bank reconciliation, and various types of current liabilities. Furthermore, it delves into financial analysis techniques, specifically the Current Ratio and Acid-Test Ratio, to assess a company's liquidity [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-1].

Key topics include:
*   **Cash**: Major activities of a business, cash and internal controls, buying and selling merchandise (discounts & returns, purchase & sales transactions), and bank reconciliation. *Petty cash will not be covered.* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-1]
*   **Current Liabilities**: Known liabilities (accrued liabilities like Sales Tax (GST) and Payroll, Short Term Notes Payable), estimated liabilities (warranty provision), and contingent liabilities [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-1].
*   **Financial Analysis**: Current Ratio and Acid Test Ratio [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-1].

### I. Statement of Financial Position & The Three Major Activities of a Business (LO1)

The Statement of Financial Position (Balance Sheet) categorizes assets, liabilities, and equity, which are directly linked to the three major activities of a business [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-2]:

*   **Operating Activities**: Associated with the primary purpose of the business.
    *   **Cash inflow**: Selling products or services.
    *   **Cash outflow**: Buying inventory for resale, incurring and paying for necessary expenses (e.g., salaries, rent, utilities) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-2].
*   **Investing Activities**: Relate to the acquisition and disposal of long-term assets.
    *   **Cash outflow**: Purchase of assets (e.g., Property, Plant, and Equipment (PPE), financial assets) for use in the business.
    *   **Cash inflow**: Disposal/sale of assets [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-2].
*   **Financing Activities**: Involve borrowing and repaying money, and raising funds from owners.
    *   **Cash inflow**: Borrowing money from creditors or raising money from investors.
    *   **Cash outflow**: Repayment of debts or distributing cash dividends to shareholders [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-2].

### II. Cash, Cash Equivalents, and Internal Controls (LO2)

**A. Cash, Cash Equivalents, and Liquidity**
*   **Liquid assets** are cash and similar assets that can be readily used to settle near-term obligations [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-2].
*   **Cash** includes currency, coins, amounts in bank accounts (checking and some savings accounts), customer checks, cashier checks, certified checks, and money orders [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-2].
*   **Cash Equivalents** are short-term, highly liquid investments that are:
    1.  Readily convertible to known amounts of cash.
    2.  Subject to an insignificant risk of changes in value [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-2].

**B. Internal Controls of Cash**
Cash is highly susceptible to theft and fraud, making effective internal control essential. An effective system should follow these guidelines [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-2]:
1.  **Separation of duties**:
    *   Complete separation of cash receipts functions and cash disbursement functions.
    *   For example, the person receiving cash should be different from the person disbursing cash.
2.  **Effective prescribed policies & procedures**:
    *   Proper cash handling procedures ensure cash receipts are promptly deposited to prevent accumulation of cash on hand.
    *   Separate approval for purchases and actual cash payments.
    *   Cash disbursements should be made by pre-numbered checks, with different individuals for approval and check-signing.
    *   Perform bank reconciliations periodically [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-2].

**C. Cash Management**
The goals of cash management are twofold [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-2]:
1.  Plan cash receipts to meet cash payments when due.
2.  Maintain a minimum level of cash necessary to operate.

Effective cash management involves applying principles such as encouraging collection of receivables, delaying payment of liabilities, keeping only necessary levels of assets, planning expenditures, and investing excess cash [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-2].

### III. Buying and Selling Merchandise: Purchases & Sales (LO3)

This section focuses on typical transactions involving merchandise, assuming a perpetual inventory system [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3].

**A. Purchase and Sales Transactions**

*   **Purchase Merchandise Example**:
    Uniqlo purchases \$10,000 worth of inventory merchandise. \$6,000 is paid in cash, with the remaining \$4,000 on credit [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3].
    | Account              | Debit   | Credit  |
    | :------------------- | :------ | :------ |
    | Inventory            | \$10,000 |         |
    | Cash                 |         | \$6,000 |
    | Accounts Payable     |         | \$4,000 |
    *To record purchase of inventory.*

    One month later, Uniqlo pays the supplier [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3]:
    | Account          | Debit  | Credit |
    | :--------------- | :----- | :----- |
    | Accounts Payable | \$4,000 |        |
    | Cash             |        | \$4,000 |
    *To record payment to supplier.*

*   **Merchandise Sales Example**:
    Uniqlo sells merchandise for \$15,000. \$3,000 is received in cash, with the remaining \$12,000 on credit. The cost of this inventory is \$10,000 [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3].

    To record the sales revenue [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3]:
    | Account            | Debit    | Credit   |
    | :----------------- | :------- | :------- |
    | Cash               | \$3,000  |          |
    | Accounts Receivable | \$12,000 |          |
    | Sales Revenue      |          | \$15,000 |
    *To record merchandise sales.*

    To record the Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3]:
    | Account   | Debit    | Credit   |
    | :-------- | :------- | :------- |
    | COGS      | \$10,000 |          |
    | Inventory |          | \$10,000 |
    *To record cost of merchandise sold.*

    Later, the customer pays Uniqlo [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3]:
    | Account            | Debit    | Credit   |
    | :----------------- | :------- | :------- |
    | Cash               | \$12,000 |          |
    | Accounts Receivable |          | \$12,000 |
    *To record customer payment.*

**B. Credit Terms**
Credit terms are often abbreviated to indicate discount percentages and payment periods, such as "2/10, n/30" [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3]:
*   **2**: Discount Percentage (e.g., 2%)
*   **10**: Number of days discount is allowed (e.g., if paid within 10 days)
*   **n/30**: Net is due within the credit period, with a maximum of 30 days in the credit period. This means the full price (net of any returns) is due in 30 days from the date of sale/invoice if the discount is not taken [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3].

**C. Purchase Discounts & Sales Discounts**

*   **Purchase Discounts Example**:
    Uniqlo purchases \$10,000 worth of inventory merchandise on credit [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3].
    | Account            | Debit    | Credit   |
    | :----------------- | :------- | :------- |
    | Inventory          | \$10,000 |          |
    | Accounts Payable   |          | \$10,000 |
    *To record purchase on credit.*

    Assuming credit terms are 5/5, n/30, and Uniqlo pays the full amount within 5 days [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3]:
    | Account            | Debit    | Credit   |
    | :----------------- | :------- | :------- |
    | Accounts Payable   | \$10,000 |          |
    | Inventory (5% x \$10k) |          | \$500    |
    | Cash               |          | \$9,500  |
    *To record payment with purchase discount.*

*   **Sales Discounts Example**:
    Uniqlo sells merchandise on credit for \$15,000 [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3].
    | Account            | Debit    | Credit   |
    | :----------------- | :------- | :------- |
    | Accounts Receivable | \$15,000 |          |
    | Sales Revenue      |          | \$15,000 |
    *To record sales on credit.*

    Assuming credit terms are 3/10, n/45, and the customer pays the full amount within 10 days [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3]:
    | Account            | Debit    | Credit   |
    | :----------------- | :------- | :------- |
    | Cash               | \$14,550 |          |
    | Sales Discount (3% x \$15k) | \$450    |          |
    | Accounts Receivable |          | \$15,000 |
    *To record customer payment with sales discount.*

**D. Purchase Returns & Sales Returns**

*   **Purchase Returns Example**:
    Uniqlo returns merchandise worth \$500 previously bought on credit to its supplier [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3].
    | Account            | Debit  | Credit |
    | :----------------- | :----- | :----- |
    | Accounts Payable   | \$500  |        |
    | Inventory          |        | \$500  |
    *To record purchase return.*

*   **Sales Returns Example**:
    A customer who bought merchandise on credit returns part of it to Uniqlo. The returned item was originally sold for \$200 and cost \$70 to Uniqlo [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3].

    To record the sales return [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3]:
    | Account            | Debit  | Credit |
    | :----------------- | :----- | :----- |
    | Sales Returns      | \$200  |        |
    | Accounts Receivable |        | \$200  |
    *To record customer return.*

    To adjust Cost of Goods Sold and Inventory [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3]:
    | Account           | Debit | Credit |
    | :---------------- | :---- | :----- |
    | Inventory         | \$70  |        |
    | Cost of Goods Sold |       | \$70   |
    *To adjust inventory for returned merchandise.*

**E. Contra-revenue & Net Sales**
Sales Discounts and Sales Returns are **contra-revenue accounts** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3].
*   They track negative adjustments to sales.
*   They reduce revenue (similar to an expense).
*   They typically have a normal debit balance.
*   On the Income Statement, they are deducted from gross revenue to arrive at **Net Sales Revenue** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3].

*   **Net Sales Revenue Example**:
    | Item                      | Amount      |
    | :------------------------ | :---------- |
    | Sales Revenue             | \$100,000   |
    | Less: Sales discounts & returns | (\$7,800)   |
    | **Net Sales**             | **\$92,200** |
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-3]

### IV. Bank Reconciliation (LO4)

**A. Controls from Bank Procedures & Basic Bank Services**
Banks provide important services that help businesses control cash by restricting access, documenting procedures, and independently verifying transactions. Basic bank services include bank accounts, signature cards, checks, electronic funds transfers, and bank statements [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-4].

**B. Bank Statement**
A **bank statement** is a monthly report from the bank summarizing account activity. It shows [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-4]:
*   Changes in the account balance.
*   Checks paid and other debits (withdrawals).
*   Deposits and other credits (additions).
*   The daily account balance.

From the bank's perspective, deposits are liabilities (credits), and customer withdrawals reduce the bank's liabilities (debits) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-4].

**C. Bank Reconciliation**
A **bank reconciliation** is an internal report prepared to verify the accuracy of both the bank statement and the cash accounts of a business or individual [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-4].

**D. Why Bank Statement Balance is NOT Equal to Cash Account?**
Differences arise due to information known by one party but not yet by the other [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-4]:

*   **Your Bank May Not Know About**:
    1.  **Errors made by the bank**.
    2.  **Time lag differences**:
        *   **Deposits in Transit**: Deposits made by you but not yet processed by the bank.
        *   **Outstanding Checks**: Checks you wrote but have not yet been processed by the bank [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-4].

*   **You May Not Know About**:
    3.  **Bank Credits**: Additions by the bank to your account (e.g., interest income).
    4.  **Bank Debits**: Deductions by the bank (e.g., bank service fees, charges).
    5.  **Direct Deposits**: Deposits made directly to your account.
    6.  **NSF Checks**: Customer checks you deposited for which the customer has insufficient funds (Non-Sufficient Funds).
    7.  **Errors made by you** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-4].

**E. Bank Reconciliation Overview: Adjustments**
The process adjusts both the bank statement balance and the book balance until they equal the "adjusted" or "true" cash balance [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-4]:

| Adjustment on the Bank Side | Adjustment on the Book Side     |
| :-------------------------- | :------------------------------ |
| **Bank Statement balance**  | **Book balance**                |
| + Deposits in transit       | + Interest paid by the bank     |
| - Outstanding checks        | + Direct deposits               |
| +/- Bank errors             | - Service charges               |
|                             | - NSF checks                    |
|                             | - Bank transfer                 |
|                             | +/- Accounting errors           |
| **Adjusted Bank Balance**   | **Adjusted Book Balance**       |
An account is reconciled when both adjusted balances are equal [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-4].

**F. VideoBuster Bank Reconciliation Example**

Here's an example of adjusting the bank and book sides [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-4]:

**Adjusting the Bank Side:**
*   Bank statement balance: \$2,050.00
*   Add: Deposit of Oct. 31 in transit: \$145.00
*   Deduct: Outstanding checks (No. 124: \$150.00, No. 126: \$200.00) = \$350.00
*   **Adjusted bank balance: \$1,845.00** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-4]

**Adjusting the Book Side:**
*   Book balance: \$1,404.58
*   Add:
    *   Collections (direct transfer of note less \$15 fee): \$485.00
    *   Interest earned: \$8.42
    *   Total additions: \$493.42
*   Deduct:
    *   Check printing charge: \$23.00
    *   NSF check plus service fee: \$30.00
    *   Total deductions: \$53.00
*   **Adjusted book balance: \$1,845.00** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-4]

Since the adjusted bank balance (\$1,845.00) equals the adjusted book balance (\$1,845.00), the account is reconciled [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-4].

**G. Adjusting Entries for the Books**
After reconciliation, adjusting entries are required for items on the book side of the reconciliation to bring the cash account up to date [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-4].

*   **Direct transfer received from customer and related bank service charge**:
    | Account             | Debit | Credit |
    | :------------------ | :---- | :----- |
    | Cash                | \$485 |        |
    | Collection Expense  | \$15  |        |
    | Notes Receivable    |       | \$500  |
    *To record note collection and bank fee.* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-4]

*   **Interest Revenue**:
    | Account        | Debit | Credit |
    | :------------- | :---- | :----- |
    | Cash           | \$8.42 |        |
    | Interest Revenue |       | \$8.42 |
    *To record interest earned.* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-4]

*   **Miscellaneous bank charges**:
    | Account               | Debit | Credit |
    | :-------------------- | :---- | :----- |
    | Miscellaneous Expenses | \$23  |        |
    | Cash                  |       | \$23   |
    *To record check printing charge.* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-4]

*   **Customer (T. Woods) check rejected as NSF plus the service fee of \$10**:
    | Account                      | Debit | Credit |
    | :--------------------------- | :---- | :----- |
    | Accounts Receivable—T. Woods | \$30  |        |
    | Cash                         |       | \$30   |
    *To charge Woods' account for NSF check and bank fee.* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-4]

### V. Current Liabilities (LO3)

**A. Liabilities (Recap)**
A liability is a present debt or obligation resulting from past transactions or events, to be paid in the future with assets or services, and measurable with reasonable precision. Liabilities are recorded at their current cash equivalent [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].

*   **Current Liabilities**: Obligations with a maturity of one year or less.
*   **Noncurrent Liabilities**: Obligations with a maturity of more than one year [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].

**B. Three Major Uncertainties in Liabilities**
Liabilities are categorized based on the uncertainty surrounding "whom to pay," "when to pay," and "how much to pay" [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]:
1.  **Known Liabilities**: Have little uncertainty; who, when, and how much is determinable.
2.  **Estimated Liabilities**: Known obligations with an uncertain amount that can be reliably estimated.
3.  **Contingent Liabilities**: Potential obligations from a past event, which are not yet effective liabilities until a future event occurs [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].

**C. Types of "Known" Current Liabilities**

| Account Name                      | Also known as      | Definition                                                                                                                    |
| :-------------------------------- | :----------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Accounts Payable                  | Trade Payable      | Obligations to pay for goods or services used in the basic operating activities of the business.                                |
| Accrued Liabilities               | Accrued Expenses   | Expenses already incurred but not yet paid (e.g., Accrued Taxes Payable, Accrued Wages).                                      |
| Deferred Revenues                 | Unearned Revenues  | Revenues not earned but company already received payment in advance. A liability until goods/services are rendered.           |
| Short Term Notes Payable          |                    | Obligations supported by a formal written contract.                                                                           |
| Current Portion of Long-term Debt |                    | Part of a long-term debt that is due within a year.                                                                           |
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

**1. Accounts Payable (RECAP)**
Amounts owed to suppliers for products or services purchased on credit in the normal course of operations [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].

*   **Purchasing inventory merchandise on credit**:
    | Account          | Debit | Credit |
    | :--------------- | :---- | :----- |
    | DR Inventory     |       |        |
    | CR Accounts Payable |       |        |

*   **Incurring operating expense on credit (e.g., repairs, utilities, fees)**:
    | Account         | Debit | Credit |
    | :-------------- | :---- | :----- |
    | DR Expense      |       |        |
    | CR Accounts Payable |       |        |
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

**2. Accrued Taxes: Sales Tax Payable (GST)**
Sales taxes (or Goods and Services Taxes (GST) in Singapore) are taxes on retail sales. Merchandise retailers collect these taxes and are obligated to pay them to taxation authorities as a current liability [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].

*   **Recording \$10,000 sale transaction with GST (7%)**:
    | Account                 | Debit     | Credit    |
    | :---------------------- | :-------- | :-------- |
    | Cash/Accounts Receivable | \$10,700  |           |
    | Sales Revenue           |           | \$10,000  |
    | GST Payable (7% x \$10k) |           | \$700     |
    *To record sales with GST collected.* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

*   **Recording payment of GST to the Inland Revenue Authority of Singapore (IRAS)**:
    | Account     | Debit | Credit |
    | :---------- | :---- | :----- |
    | GST Payable | \$700 |        |
    | Cash        |       | \$700  |
    *To record payment of GST.* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

**3. Payroll Liabilities: CPF contributions**
Employers incur payroll expenses and liabilities from having employees. These include amounts withheld from employee wages (e.g., employee's CPF contribution) and amounts borne by the employer (e.g., employer's CPF contribution) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].

*   **Employee's CPF contribution (e.g., 20% on \$10,000 salaries expense)**:
    | Account                   | Debit    | Credit   |
    | :------------------------ | :------- | :------- |
    | Salaries Expense          | \$10,000 |          |
    | Salaries Payable – to employee |          | \$8,000  |
    | CPF Payable – employee contribution |          | \$2,000  |
    *To record salaries and employee CPF withheld.* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

*   **Employer's CPF contribution (e.g., 17% on \$10,000 salaries expense)**:
    | Account                   | Debit    | Credit   |
    | :------------------------ | :------- | :------- |
    | Payroll Expense – CPF     | \$1,700  |          |
    | CPF Payable – employer contribution |          | \$1,700  |
    *To record employer's CPF contribution.* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

**4. Unearned Revenue (RECAP)**
Amounts received in advance from customers for future products or services [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].

*   **Receiving payment in advance of \$12,000 for goods/services yet to be rendered**:
    | Account         | Debit    | Credit   |
    | :-------------- | :------- | :------- |
    | Cash            | \$12,000 |          |
    | Unearned Revenues |          | \$12,000 |
    *To record cash received in advance.* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

*   **Recognizing revenue upon delivery of half the goods/services (i.e., \$6,000)**:
    | Account         | Debit  | Credit |
    | :-------------- | :----- | :----- |
    | Unearned Revenues | \$6,000 |        |
    | Sales Revenue   |        | \$6,000 |
    *To record earned revenue (adjusting journal entry).* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

**5. Short-Term (ST) Notes Payable**
A formal written promise to pay a specified amount on a definite future date within one year [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].
*   For the lender, interest is revenue.
*   For the borrower, interest is an expense.
*   **Interest = Principal × Interest Rate × Time Period** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].
    *   *Note: Use 360 days for one year when calculating daily interest, then multiply by the actual number of days the note is outstanding.* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

*   **Example: Lenovo borrows \$100,000 for 60 days at an annual interest rate of 12%**:
    Interest = \$100,000 × 12% × (60/360) = \$2,000 [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].

*   **Logic Co Example: Borrowing on Feb 1st, 2020, \$7,000 at 10% interest for 180 days**:
    | Account                  | Debit  | Credit |
    | :----------------------- | :----- | :----- |
    | Cash                     | \$7,000 |        |
    | Notes Payable – Generous Co. |        | \$7,000 |
    *To record borrowing.* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

*   **When Logic repays the note plus interest**:
    Interest Expense = 10% x \$7,000 x (180/360) = \$350
    | Account                  | Debit  | Credit |
    | :----------------------- | :----- | :----- |
    | Notes Payable – Generous Company | \$7,000 |        |
    | Interest Expense         | \$350  |        |
    | Cash                     |        | \$7,350 |
    *To record repayment of note and interest.* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

*   **End-of-Period Adjustments Example: Logic Company borrows \$9,600 from a bank at 15% interest for 120 days on Nov 16th, 2020.**
    At Dec 31st, 2020, an adjusting entry is needed for 45 days of interest (14 days in Nov + 31 days in Dec) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]:
    Interest Expense = 15% × \$9,600 × (45/360) = \$180
    | Account         | Debit | Credit |
    | :-------------- | :---- | :----- |
    | Interest Expense | \$180 |        |
    | Interest Payable |       | \$180  |
    *To accrue interest expense at year-end.* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

    On March 16th, 2021, Logic repays the note principal plus interest. Remaining days of interest are 75 (120 - 45 = 75) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]:
    Interest Expense = 15% × \$9,600 × (75/360) = \$300
    | Account         | Debit    | Credit   |
    | :-------------- | :------- | :------- |
    | Interest Expense | \$300    |          |
    | Interest Payable | \$180    |          |
    | Notes Payable   | \$9,600  |          |
    | Cash            |          | \$10,080 |
    *To record repayment of note and interest.* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

**D. Estimated Liabilities: Warranty**
Estimated liabilities are known obligations of an uncertain amount that can be reliably estimated [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].
*   **Warranty Liabilities**: The seller's obligation to replace or correct a product or service that fails to perform as expected within a specified period [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].
*   To comply with the **full disclosure** and **matching principles**, sellers report expected warranty expense in the period when revenue from the sale is reported.
*   Warranty expense is an estimation, often based on past experience [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].

*   **Rimowa Example: On Nov 15th, 2020, Rimowa sold luggage for \$50,000 with a 5-year warranty. Warranty expenses average about 3% of the selling price.**
    Warranty Expense = 3% × \$50,000 = \$1,500
    | Account           | Debit    | Credit   |
    | :---------------- | :------- | :------- |
    | Warranty Expense  | \$1,500  |          |
    | Warranty Provision |          | \$1,500  |
    *To record estimated warranty expense.* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

*   **On Aug 24th, 2021, a customer returns luggage for repairs, costing Rimowa \$120 in replacement parts.**
    | Account           | Debit | Credit |
    | :---------------- | :---- | :----- |
    | Warranty Provision | \$120 |        |
    | Parts Inventory   |       | \$120  |
    *To record warranty repair using provision.* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

**E. Contingent Liabilities**
Contingent liabilities are potential liabilities created by a past event that are not effective liabilities until some future event happens (e.g., lawsuits, litigation, debt guarantees) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].

**When to record a contingent liability**:

| Probability of Occurrence | Estimable     | Non-Estimable |
| :------------------------ | :------------ | :------------ |
| **Probable**              | Record as Liability | Disclose in Notes |
| **Reasonably Possible**   | Disclose in Notes | Disclose in Notes |
| **Remote**                | No disclosure needed | No disclosure needed |
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

*   **Probable**: High chance of occurrence (>50% IFRS, >70% GAAP).
*   **Reasonably Possible**: More than remote but less than likely.
*   **Remote**: Slight chance of occurrence [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].

### VI. Financial Analysis

**A. Current Ratio**
The Current Ratio measures a company's ability to pay its short-term obligations with short-term assets [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].

$$ \text{Current Ratio} = \frac{\text{Current Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}} $$
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

*   A higher ratio generally indicates better liquidity (how easily an asset can be converted to cash) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].
*   However, a ratio that is too high might suggest inefficient use of resources [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].

*   **Example: ComfortDelGro**
    *   2021 Current Assets: \$1,588 million
    *   2021 Current Liabilities: \$990 million
    *   **2021 Current Ratio**: \$1,588 / \$990 = **1.60** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

*   **Comparison: ComfortDelGro vs. Uber**
    *   Uber's current ratio in 2019 was significantly higher than ComfortDelGro (2.47 vs. 1.18), largely due to a high amount of cash raised from its IPO and debt issuance [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].
    *   In 2020, both companies had similar liquidity (around 1.4).
    *   In 2021, Uber's ratio dropped below 1 (0.98), while ComfortDelGro's increased to 1.60. Uber's decline was attributed to significant net cash outflows from operating and investing activities, and increasing current liabilities [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].

**B. Acid-Test Ratio (Quick Ratio)**
The Acid-Test Ratio measures a company's ability to quickly pay its short-term obligations using only its most liquid assets [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].

$$ \text{Acid-Test Ratio} = \frac{\text{Quick Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}} $$
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

*   **Quick Assets** include Cash + Short-term investments + Current trade receivables. This ratio excludes less liquid current assets like inventory, prepaids, and other current assets [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].
*   Also known as the "Quick Ratio," it is useful for assessing potential near-term liquidity problems [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].
*   A common rule of thumb suggests an acid-test ratio of at least 1.0 indicates a company is unlikely to face liquidity problems in the near future [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].

*   **Example: ComfortDelGro**
    *   2021 Quick Assets: \$1,301 million (Cash + short-term deposits + trade receivables)
    *   2021 Current Liabilities: \$990 million
    *   **2021 Acid-Test Ratio**: \$1,301 / \$990 = **1.31** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5]

*   **Comparison: ComfortDelGro vs. Uber**
    *   Uber's quick ratio in 2019 was also more than double ComfortDelGro's, benefiting from IPO proceeds [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].
    *   However, Uber experienced significant net cash outflows in 2020 and 2021, leading to a large drop in its quick assets and an increase in current liabilities. Its acid-test ratio declined to 0.75 in 2021.
    *   ComfortDelGro's acid-test ratio, conversely, showed improvement from 2019 to 2021, with increasing quick assets and decreasing current liabilities [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-07-slides-cash-and-current-liabilities-pdf#section-5].
