## Concepts of Inventory

### Merchandising Companies

**Service Companies** earn revenues by selling services ("time") [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-5].
**Merchandising Companies**, on the other hand, earn revenues by selling products or goods [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-5]. Their supply chain typically involves manufacturers, wholesalers, retailers, and ultimately, customers. The focus in this module is primarily on the merchandiser's role [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-5].

### What is Inventory?

Inventories are classified as assets and include [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-6]:
*   Goods held for sale in the ordinary course of business.
*   Goods in the process of production for such sale.
*   Materials or supplies to be consumed in the production process or in the rendering of services.

Under SFRS(I) 1-2, inventories encompass goods purchased and held for resale (e.g., merchandise by a retailer), finished goods, work in progress, and materials/supplies awaiting use. The primary focus of this lecture is on goods held for sale in the ordinary course of business [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-6].

#### Importance of Inventories for Merchandisers
Inventories are crucial for merchandisers because they represent [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-7]:
*   One of the largest assets on their Statement of Financial Position/Balance Sheet.
*   One of the largest expenses (Cost of Goods Sold - COGS) on their Income Statement.
This means inventories and COGS significantly impact a merchandiser's financial position and profitability [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-7].

#### Examples of Inventory in Different Companies
*   **NTUC FairPrice**: In 2021, NTUC FairPrice reported Inventories of $291,264,000 as a current asset and Inventories consumed (which is COGS) of ($3,043,635,000) on their financial statements [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-8].
*   **Sasa**: For 2022, Sasa reported Inventories of HK$747,946,000 as current assets and Cost of Sales of (HK$2,152,181,000) on their financial statements [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-9].
*   **CapitaLand (Property Developer)**: For a property developer like CapitaLand, "inventory" includes "development properties for sale and stocks," which covers properties under development (for revenue recognized over time or at a point in time), completed development properties, and consumable stocks [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-10]. This illustrates how the definition of inventory adapts to the nature of the business.

### Flow of Inventory Costs

The flow of inventory costs involves three main stages [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-7]:
*   **Stage 1: Purchasing/Production Activities**: When merchandise is purchased or produced, its cost is initially recorded.
*   **Stage 2: Additions to Inventory on the Balance Sheet**: The cost of purchased or produced merchandise becomes part of "Merchandise Inventory," an asset on the balance sheet.
*   **Stage 3: Sale - Cost of Goods Sold on Income Statement**: When the merchandise is sold, its cost is transferred from inventory to "Cost of Goods Sold," an expense on the income statement.

### What's Counted in Inventory?

Merchandise inventory includes all goods that a company owns and holds for sale, regardless of their physical location when inventory is counted [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-11]. Special attention is required for:

1.  **Goods in Transit**: Ownership of goods in transit depends on the shipping terms [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-11, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-12]:
    *   **FOB (Free On Board) Shipping Point**: Ownership transfers from seller to buyer at the shipping point. The buyer pays transportation costs, and the goods are reported as part of the **buyer's inventory** in transit [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-12].
    *   **FOB Destination**: Ownership transfers from seller to buyer at the destination. The seller pays transportation costs, and the goods are reported as part of the **seller's inventory** in transit until they reach the buyer [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-12].

2.  **Goods on Consignment**: These are goods that a company (the **consignor**) owns but are on display for sale at another business (the **consignee**) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-13]. Ownership remains with the consignor, meaning these goods are still part of the consignor's inventory, not the consignee's [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-13].

3.  **Goods Damaged or Obsolete**: Damaged or obsolete goods are included in inventory if they can still be sold, but they must be reported at their **net realizable value** (NRV) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-11]. If they cannot be sold, they are removed from inventory and recognized as a loss.

### Cost of Inventory

According to SFRS(I) 1-2, the cost of inventories includes all costs of purchase, cost of conversion, and other costs incurred in bringing the inventories to their present location and condition [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-14].
For merchandising companies, inventory is recorded at **cost**, which comprises all expenditures necessary to bring an item to a sellable condition and location. These costs typically include [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-14]:
*   Invoice cost (purchase price).
*   Freight/Transportation cost (inward freight).
*   Insurance cost (during shipment).
*   Storage cost (during shipment).
*   Import taxes/duties.
*   **Less**: Any purchase discounts/returns.

**Note**: Costs incurred *after* inventory is ready for use are *not* included in inventory cost (e.g., marketing costs, salesperson salaries, financing costs, warehouse costs, retail store costs) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-14].

## Inventory Systems

Two primary systems are used to account for inventory: the perpetual inventory system and the periodic inventory system [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-4, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-15].

### Perpetual Inventory System

*   **Record Keeping**: Maintains an up-to-date record of inventory and cost of goods sold (COGS) for each item [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-15].
*   **Inventory Purchases**: Directly added to the `Inventory` account [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-15].
*   **Transaction Recording**: Records transactions on a continuous, transaction-by-transaction basis throughout the period [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-15].
*   **Information Availability**: Information on COGS and ending inventory is typically available continuously [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-15].

#### Perpetual System: Purchasing and Selling Inventory

Under the perpetual system, the `Inventory` account is continually updated for merchandising transactions [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-16].

**Example 1: Purchase of Inventory**
Skye Company bought merchandise inventory for $5,000 cash and $3,000 on credit [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-16].
| Account             | Debit   | Credit  |
| :------------------ | :------ | :------ |
| Inventory           | $8,000  |         |
| Cash                |         | $5,000  |
| Accounts Payable    |         | $3,000  |

**Example 2: Sale of Inventory**
Skye Company sold $9,000 of merchandise on credit. The merchandise cost $4,800 [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-16].
Two entries are required:
1.  **To record the sale**:
    | Account            | Debit   | Credit  |
    | :----------------- | :------ | :------ |
    | Accounts Receivable| $9,000  |         |
    | Sales Revenue      |         | $9,000  |
2.  **To record the cost of goods sold**:
    | Account            | Debit   | Credit  |
    | :----------------- | :------ | :------ |
    | Cost of Goods Sold | $4,800  |         |
    | Inventory          |         | $4,800  |

Sales and COGS are typically recorded as normal journal entries at the time of transaction [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-16].

### Periodic Inventory System

*   **Record Keeping**: No up-to-date record of inventory during the accounting period [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-15].
*   **Inventory Purchases**: Recorded in a temporary account called "`Purchases`," not directly added to the `Inventory` account [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-15].
*   **Transaction Recording**: A physical count of inventory is performed at the end of the period [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-15].
*   **COGS Calculation**: COGS is calculated indirectly using the COGS equation at the end of the period [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-15].

#### Periodic System: COGS Equation

The Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) equation is central to the periodic system [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-17]:
`Beginning Inventory + Net Purchases = Cost of Goods Available for Sale`
`Cost of Goods Available for Sale - Ending Inventory = Cost of Goods Sold`
Therefore, `COGS = Beginning Inventory + Net Purchases – Ending Inventory` [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-17].

#### Periodic System: Recording Purchases & Deriving COGS

Under the periodic system, purchases are recorded in a temporary "Purchases" account, which is closed to the `Inventory` account at period-end [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-18]. COGS is derived indirectly from the COGS equation [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-18].

**Example: ReadMe Magazines Co.**
1.  **Purchase**: ReadMe Magazines Co. purchased $7,000 of magazines for resale [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-18].
    | Account          | Debit   | Credit  |
    | :--------------- | :------ | :------ |
    | Purchases        | $7,000  |         |
    | Cash/Accounts Payable|         | $7,000  |

2.  **Deriving COGS**: ReadMe uses the COGS equation at period-end [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-18].
    *   Beginning Inventory Balance: $10,000
    *   Add: Net Purchases: $7,000
    *   Less: Cost of Goods Sold: ?
    *   End Inventory (physical stock count): $8,000
    *   `COGS = $10,000 + $7,000 – $8,000 = $9,000` [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-18].

### Decathlon Example: Perpetual vs. Periodic Comparison

This example illustrates the differences between perpetual and periodic systems for various transactions.

#### 1. Merchandise Purchasing
On Sept 1, Decathlon purchased 800 footballs @$10 each on credit from its supplier ($8,000 total) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-19].

**Perpetual System**:
*   `Inventory` account is debited directly.
    | Account           | Debit   | Credit  |
    | :---------------- | :------ | :------ |
    | Inventory         | $8,000  |         |
    | Accounts Payable  |         | $8,000  |
*   Merchandise Inventory balance: $8,000 [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-19].

**Periodic System**:
*   `Purchases` (a temporary account) is debited.
    | Account           | Debit   | Credit  |
    | :---------------- | :------ | :------ |
    | Purchases         | $8,000  |         |
    | Accounts Payable  |         | $8,000  |
*   No direct update to the `Inventory` balance at this time; the balance is in the `Purchases` account [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-19].

#### 2. Transportation Costs (Freight-in)
On Sept 5, Decathlon purchased 320 more footballs @$10 each on credit with terms FOB shipping point. Transportation charge of $250 is paid in cash [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-20].

**Ownership & Transportation Costs**:
*   **FOB Shipping Point**: Ownership transfers to the buyer at the shipping point. Transportation costs are paid by the buyer and included in the buyer's inventory cost [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-20].
*   **FOB Destination**: Ownership transfers to the buyer at the destination. Transportation costs are paid by the seller and recorded as a selling expense in the seller's accounts [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-20].

**Perpetual System**:
*   Both the cost of goods ($3,200) and freight-in ($250) are added to `Inventory`.
    | Account           | Debit   | Credit  |
    | :---------------- | :------ | :------ |
    | Inventory         | $3,450  |         |
    | Accounts Payable  |         | $3,200  |
    | Cash              |         | $250    |
*   Merchandise Inventory balance: $8,000 (previous) + $3,450 = $11,450 [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-20].

**Periodic System**:
*   The cost of goods is debited to `Purchases`, and freight-in is debited to a separate `Freight-in` account.
    | Account           | Debit   | Credit  |
    | :---------------- | :------ | :------ |
    | Purchases         | $3,200  |         |
    | Freight-in        | $250    |         |
    | Accounts Payable  |         | $3,200  |
    | Cash              |         | $250    |
*   `Purchases` account balance: $8,000 (previous) + $3,200 = $11,200 [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-20].

#### 3. Returns
On Sept 6, Decathlon returns 140 footballs (bought at $10 each previously) to its supplier ($1,400 total) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-21].

**Perpetual System**:
*   `Accounts Payable` is debited, and `Inventory` is credited to reflect the reduction.
    | Account           | Debit   | Credit  |
    | :---------------- | :------ | :------ |
    | Accounts Payable  | $1,400  |         |
    | Inventory         |         | $1,400  |
*   Merchandise Inventory balance: $11,450 (previous) - $1,400 = $10,050. Decathlon now has 980 footballs (800 + 320 - 140) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-21].

**Periodic System**:
*   `Accounts Payable` is debited, and `Purchase Returns` (a temporary contra-purchases account) is credited.
    | Account          | Debit   | Credit  |
    | :--------------- | :------ | :------ |
    | Accounts Payable | $1,400  |         |
    | Purchase Returns |         | $1,400  |
*   The `Purchases` balance remains $11,200; the return is in the temporary `Purchase Returns` account [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-21].

#### 4. Discounts
For both Sept 1 and Sept 5 purchases, the credit term is 2/10, n/30 (2% discount if paid within 10 days, otherwise full amount due in 30 days). Decathlon pays both invoices on Sept 8 (within 10 days) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-22].

*   Invoice total (after returns) = $8,000 (Sept 1) + $3,200 (Sept 5) - $1,400 (returns) = $9,800.
*   Discount = 2% of $9,800 = $196.
*   Cash payment = $9,800 - $196 = $9,604 [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-22].

**Perpetual System**:
*   `Accounts Payable` is debited for the full amount, `Cash` is credited for the net payment, and `Inventory` is credited for the discount amount.
    | Account           | Debit   | Credit  |
    | :---------------- | :------ | :------ |
    | Accounts Payable  | $9,800  |         |
    | Inventory         |         | $196    |
    | Cash              |         | $9,604  |
*   Merchandise Inventory balance: $10,050 (previous) - $196 = $9,854 [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-22].

**Periodic System**:
*   `Accounts Payable` is debited for the full amount, `Cash` is credited for the net payment, and `Purchase Discounts` (a temporary contra-purchases account) is credited for the discount.
    | Account           | Debit   | Credit  |
    | :---------------- | :------ | :------ |
    | Accounts Payable  | $9,800  |         |
    | Purchase Discounts|         | $196    |
    | Cash              |         | $9,604  |
*   The `Purchases` balance remains $11,200; the discount is in the temporary `Purchase Discounts` account [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-22].

#### Decathlon's Cost of Inventory (Perpetual System)

After all purchases, freight, returns, and discounts, Decathlon's total cost for the footballs is calculated as [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-23]:
*   Total purchase price: ($8,000 + $3,200) = $11,200
*   Plus: Freight-in: $250
*   Less: Purchase returns (140 footballs): ($1,400)
*   Less: Purchase discounts: ($196)
*   **Total cost of footballs (980 footballs)**: **$9,854**
*   **Cost per football**: $9,854 ÷ 980 = $10.06 per football [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-23].

#### 5. Merchandise Sales
Decathlon sold 500 footballs for $14 each on credit, for a total of $7,000. The cost of each football is $10.06 [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-24].

**Perpetual System**:
*   **To record the sale**:
    | Account            | Debit   | Credit  |
    | :----------------- | :------ | :------ |
    | Accounts Receivable| $7,000  |         |
    | Sales Revenue      |         | $7,000  |
*   **To record the cost of goods sold**:
    | Account            | Debit   | Credit  |
    | :----------------- | :------ | :------ |
    | Cost of Goods Sold | $5,030  |         |
    | Inventory          |         | $5,030  |
    *   (Cost of Goods Sold = 500 footballs * $10.06 = $5,030)
*   Merchandise Inventory balance: $9,854 (previous) - $5,030 = $4,824. There are now 480 footballs left in the inventory record [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-24].

**Periodic System**:
*   **To record the sale**:
    | Account            | Debit   | Credit  |
    | :----------------- | :------ | :------ |
    | Accounts Receivable| $7,000  |         |
    | Sales Revenue      |         | $7,000  |
*   Under the periodic system, the exact number of units sold is not known at the time of sale, only the sales amount. COGS cannot be recorded until a physical inventory count is performed [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-24].

#### Decathlon Example: Deriving Net Purchases & COGS (Periodic System)

At the end of the period, the temporary accounts related to purchases, discounts, returns, and freight are closed to the inventory account to determine "Net Purchases" [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-25]:
*   Purchases: $11,200
*   Less: Purchase Returns: ($1,400)
*   Less: Purchase Discounts: ($196)
*   Plus: Freight-in: $250
*   **Net Purchases**: **$9,854** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-25].

Assuming Decathlon had no beginning inventory, and a physical inventory count reveals 468 footballs (not yet sold) remain, with an inventory value of $4,706 (468 units * $9,854/980 total cost) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-25]:
*   `COGS = Beginning Inventory + Net Purchases – Ending Inventory`
*   `COGS = $0 + $9,854 – $4,706 = $5,148` [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-25].

**Journal entry to record COGS at period-end**:
| Account            | Debit   | Credit  |
| :----------------- | :------ | :------ |
| Cost of Goods Sold | $5,148  |         |
| Inventory          |         | $5,148  |

#### Perpetual System: Adjustment for Inventory Shrinkage

A merchandiser using a perpetual system needs to adjust the `Inventory` account for losses due to theft, deterioration, or errors (known as shrinkage) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-26].
*   If Decathlon's system shows 480 balls, but a physical count reveals only 468, then 12 footballs are missing (lost/stolen) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-26].
*   Shrinkage cost = 12 footballs * $10.06/football = $120.72.

**Journal entry to record inventory shrinkage**:
| Account            | Debit   | Credit  |
| :----------------- | :------ | :------ |
| Cost of Goods Sold | $120.72 |         |
| Inventory          |         | $120.72 |

**Advantages of Perpetual System**: A physical count helps confirm system amounts or highlight shortages, which is not possible under the periodic system [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-26].

## Inventory Costing Methods

When prices of goods are constantly changing, the specific cost of each item sold affects the Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) and the value of ending inventory [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-27]. There are four main inventory costing methods:
1.  Specific Identification Method
2.  FIFO (First-In, First-Out)
3.  LIFO (Last-In, First-Out) – *Not accepted under IFRS!*
4.  Weighted Average Cost Method [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-27]

### 1) Specific Identification Method

This method assigns the specific cost of each unit sold to COGS [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-28].
*   It is practical when dealing with expensive, unique, and distinguishable items where costs can be easily tracked (e.g., houses, fine jewelry, custom cars) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-28].
*   It is impractical for large quantities of similar, low-cost items (e.g., toothpaste, clothing) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-28].

### 2) FIFO (First-In, First-Out)

Under FIFO, it is assumed that the first goods purchased are the first ones sold [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-29].
*   This means that ending inventory consists of the goods most recently purchased [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-32].

#### FIFO: Computers, Inc. Example

Computers, Inc. has the following inventory information and uses the FIFO method [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-30]:
*   Beginning Inventory: 1,000 units @ $5.25 = $5,250
*   Purchases:
    *   Jan 3: 500 units @ $5.30 = $2,650
    *   June 20: 300 units @ $5.60 = $1,680
    *   Sept 15: 250 units @ $5.80 = $1,450
    *   Nov 29: 200 units @ $5.90 = $1,180
*   Total Goods Available for Sale: 2,250 units, $12,210
*   Units Sold on December 1st: 1,050 units [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-30, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-33].

**a) How much is COGS using the FIFO method?**
Since the "first ones in are the first ones out," COGS will be based on the oldest inventory [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-31]:
*   1,000 units from Beginning Inventory @ $5.25 = $5,250
*   50 units from Jan 3 purchases @ $5.30 = $265
*   **Total COGS (1,050 units)**: **$5,515** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-31, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-33].

**b) How much is left in ending inventory?**
Ending inventory consists of the most recently purchased goods [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-32]:
*   450 units remaining from Jan 3 purchases @ $5.30 = $2,385 (500-50 units sold)
*   300 units from June 20 purchases @ $5.60 = $1,680
*   250 units from Sept 15 purchases @ $5.80 = $1,450
*   200 units from Nov 29 purchases @ $5.90 = $1,180
*   **Total Ending Inventory (1,200 units)**: **$6,695** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-32, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-33].

### 3) LIFO (Last-In, First-Out) - *Not accepted under IFRS*

Under LIFO, it is assumed that the last goods purchased are the first ones sold [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-34].
*   Ending inventory consists of the goods first purchased [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-37].
*   **Important**: LIFO is NOT accepted under IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-27, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-34, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-43].

#### LIFO: Computers, Inc. Example

Using the same Computers, Inc. inventory data, but applying LIFO [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-35]:
*   Beginning Inventory: 1,000 units @ $5.25 = $5,250
*   Purchases:
    *   Jan 3: 500 units @ $5.30 = $2,650
    *   June 20: 300 units @ $5.60 = $1,680
    *   Sept 15: 250 units @ $5.80 = $1,450
    *   Nov 29: 200 units @ $5.90 = $1,180
*   Total Goods Available for Sale: 2,250 units, $12,210
*   Units Sold on December 1st: 1,050 units [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-35].

**a) How much is COGS using the LIFO method?**
Since the "last ones in are the first ones out," COGS will be based on the most recent inventory [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-36]:
*   200 units from Nov 29 purchases @ $5.90 = $1,180
*   250 units from Sept 15 purchases @ $5.80 = $1,450
*   300 units from June 20 purchases @ $5.60 = $1,680
*   300 units from Jan 3 purchases @ $5.30 = $1,590
*   **Total COGS (1,050 units)**: **$5,900** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-36, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-38].

**b) How much is left in ending inventory?**
Ending inventory consists of the earliest purchased goods [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-37]:
*   1,000 units from Beginning Inventory @ $5.25 = $5,250
*   200 units remaining from Jan 3 purchases @ $5.30 = $1,060 (500-300 units sold)
*   **Total Ending Inventory (1,200 units)**: **$6,310** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-37, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-38].

### 4) Average Cost Method (Weighted Average Cost Method)

Under the average cost method, when a unit is sold, the average cost per unit in inventory is assigned to COGS [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-39].
*   The average cost per unit is calculated by dividing the Cost of Goods Available for Sale by the Number of Units Available for Sale [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-39].

#### Average Cost: Computers, Inc. Example

Using the same Computers, Inc. inventory data, but applying the average cost method [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-40]:
*   Total Goods Available for Sale: $12,210 (2,250 units) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-40].
*   Weighted Average Cost = $12,210 / 2,250 units = $5.42667 per unit [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-40].

*   Units Sold on December 1st: 1,050 units
*   Units Remaining in Ending Inventory: 1,200 units

**a) How much is COGS using the Average Cost method?**
*   COGS = 1,050 units * $5.42667 = $5,698 (rounded) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-40].

**b) How much is left in ending inventory?**
*   Ending Inventory = 1,200 units * $5.42667 = $6,512 (rounded) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-40].

### Comparison of Methods: Effect on Income Statement and Financial Statements

Different inventory costing methods can significantly impact COGS, gross profit, income before taxes, and net income [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-38, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-41].

#### Effect on Income Statement (Computers, Inc. Example)
| Line Item                         | FIFO       | LIFO       | Weighted Average |
| :-------------------------------- | :--------- | :--------- | :--------------- |
| Net Sales                         | $25,000    | $25,000    | $25,000          |
| Merchandise Inventory, Beginning  | $5,250     | $5,250     | $5,250           |
| Net Purchases                     | $6,960     | $6,960     | $6,960           |
| Goods Available for Sale          | $12,210    | $12,210    | $12,210          |
| Merchandise Inventory, Ending     | $6,695     | $6,310     | $6,512           |
| **Cost of Goods Sold**            | **$5,515** | **$5,900** | **$5,698**       |
| **Gross Profit**                  | **$19,485**| **$19,100**| **$19,302**      |
| Operating Expenses                | $750       | $750       | $750             |
| Income before Taxes               | $18,735    | $18,350    | $18,552          |
| Income Taxes Expense (30%)        | $5,621     | $5,505     | $5,566           |
| **Net Income**                    | **$13,114**| **$12,845**| **$12,986**      |
*   *Note: Different inventory costing methods can result in very different COGS and Net Income figures.* [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-38, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-41].

#### Effect on Financial Statements (General Principles)

*   **In periods of rising costs**:
    *   **FIFO** will yield the *lowest* COGS (using older, lower costs), resulting in *higher* net income [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-42].
    *   **LIFO** will yield the *highest* COGS (using newer, higher costs), resulting in *lower* net income [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-42].
    *   **Weighted Average** will produce a COGS amount that falls between FIFO and LIFO [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-42].
*   **In periods of declining costs**:
    *   **FIFO** will yield the *highest* COGS (using older, higher costs), resulting in *lower* net income [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-42].
    *   **LIFO** will yield the *lowest* COGS (using newer, lower costs), resulting in *higher* net income [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-42].
    *   **Weighted Average** will produce a COGS amount that typically falls between FIFO and LIFO [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-42].

#### Advantages of Different Methods

| Method              | Advantage                                                                                             |
| :------------------ | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **FIFO**            | Ending inventory approximates current replacement cost.                                               |
| **LIFO**            | Better matches current costs in COGS with current revenues.                                           |
| **Weighted Average**| Smoothes out price changes.                                                                           |
*   *Source:* [acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-43]

Companies choose their inventory costing method based on net income and income tax effects, but they must use it on a **consistent basis** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-43].
**Note**: U.S. GAAP allows all methods, but **IFRS does NOT allow the use of LIFO** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-43].

#### Real-World Examples of Costing Methods
*   **NTUC FairPrice**: Measures inventories at the lower of cost and net realisable value, with cost based on the **weighted average method** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-44].
*   **Takashimaya**: Measures inventories at cost (book value reduced for profitability declines). It uses different methods for various inventory types [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-44]:
    *   Merchandise: Principally retail method and specific identification method.
    *   Products: Principally first-in, first-out method.
    *   Work in process: Principally specific identification method.
    *   Raw materials: Principally first-in, first-out method.
    *   Supplies: Principally first-in, first-out method.

## Valuation of Inventory: Lower of Cost or Net Realizable Value (LCNRV)

Ending inventory must be reported at the **lower of its cost or net realizable value (NRV)** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-45]. This means if the replacement cost of the goods is lower than their inventory cost, the market value (NRV) must be reported instead [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-45].

**Net Realizable Value (NRV)** is defined as the estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business, less the estimated costs of completion and the estimated costs necessary to make the sale [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-45].

NRV can be applied in two ways [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-45]:
1.  Separately to each individual item.
2.  To major categories of assets.

#### Dell Inc. Example: Inventory Write-down

Dell Inc. has the following ending inventory [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-46]:
*   **Intel Chips**: 10,000 units, Cost/item: $200, NRV/item: $150
*   **Seagate Drives**: 8,000 units, Cost/item: $100, NRV/item: $120

**Application of LCNRV**:
*   **Intel Chips**: Cost is $200/item, NRV is $150/item. The lower value is $150.
    *   Ending Inventory at LCNRV = 10,000 units * $150 = $1,500,000 (or $1.5M) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-46].
    *   Original cost of Intel Chips was $2,000,000 (10,000 units * $200) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-46].
    *   Dell must recognize a $0.5M ($2.0M - $1.5M) **inventory write-down** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-46].
*   **Seagate Drives**: Cost is $100/item, NRV is $120/item. The lower value is $100.
    *   Ending Inventory at LCNRV = 8,000 units * $100 = $800,000 [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-46]. Dell reports Seagate drives inventory at cost since cost is lower than NRV.

**Recording the Write-down**:
The inventory write-down is reported as part of COGS [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-46]:
| Account                          | Debit     | Credit    |
| :------------------------------- | :-------- | :-------- |
| Cost of Goods Sold               | $500,000  |           |
| Allowance for Inventory Write-down |           | $500,000  |
*   The `Allowance for Inventory Write-down` is a contra-asset account to `Inventory` [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-46].

#### Inventory Write-down: COGS or Impairment Expense?
Inventory is reported net of the `Inventory` account and the `Allowance for Inventory Write-down` on the Statement of Financial Position [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-47].
Differences exist in practice for recording write-downs [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-47]:
1.  Can be recorded as part of COGS.
2.  Can be recorded separately as an expense (e.g., Impairment Loss on inventory), especially for "obsolescence."

#### Sasa Example: Inventory & Provision (Allowance for Write-downs)
Sasa's accounting policy states that inventories are measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value, with costs assigned on a weighted-average basis [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-48].
*   In 2022, Sasa made a provision of HK$8,950,000 for slow-moving inventories and shrinkage (HK$43,317,000 in 2021) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-48]. This provision reduces the inventory value to its net realisable value.

## Financial Statement Analysis (FSA)

Financial ratios are used to assess a company's efficiency and liquidity related to inventory [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-4, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-50, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-51].

### Inventory Turnover

*   **Formula**: `Inventory Turnover = COGS / Average Inventory`
*   **Purpose**: Measures how many times a company sells (turns over) its inventory during a period. It helps assess if the company is managing its inventory well [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-51].

### Number of Days' Sales in Inventory

*   **Formula**: `Number of Days' Sales in Inventory = 365 / Inventory Turnover`
*   **Purpose**: Measures how many days, on average, it will take to convert inventory into cash or accounts receivable. It indicates how much inventory is available in terms of days' sales [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-51].

#### Example: NTUC FairPrice (2021)
*   COGS: $3,040,635,000
*   Beginning Inventory (2020 Ending Inv): $356,611,000
*   Ending Inventory (2021): $291,264,000
*   Average Inventory = ($356,611,000 + $298,264,000) / 2 = $323,938,000 [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-52].

*   **Inventory Turnover**: $3,040,635,000 / $323,938,000 = **9.39 times** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-52].
*   **Days' Sales in Inventory**: 365 / 9.39 = **38.89 days** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-52].

#### Example: Sasa (2022)
*   Cost of Sales: HK$2,152,181,000
*   Beginning Inventory (2021 Ending Inv): HK$766,107,000
*   Ending Inventory (2022): HK$747,946,000
*   Average Inventory = (HK$766,107,000 + HK$747,946,000) / 2 = HK$757,027,000 [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-53].

*   **Inventory Turnover**: HK$2,152,181,000 / HK$757,027,000 = **2.84 times** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-53].
*   **Days' Sales in Inventory**: 365 / 2.84 = **128.39 days** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-53].

### Operating Cycle of a Company

The operating cycle represents the total time it takes from purchasing inventory to collecting cash from customers [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-54]. It combines:
*   **Number of Days' Sales in Inventory**: Days to convert inventory to sales.
*   **[[lecture-05#average-collection-period|Average Collection Period]]**: Days to collect accounts receivable (from [[lecture-05]]) [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-54].

**Example: NTUC FairPrice's Operating Cycle in 2021**
*   Days' Sales in Inventory: 38.89 days
*   Average Collection Period: 3.83 days
*   Operating Cycle = 38.89 + 3.83 = **42.72 days** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-54].

### Number of Days' Purchases in Accounts Payable

*   **Formula**: `Number of Days' Purchases in AP = 365 / (Purchases / Average Accounts Payable)`
*   **Purpose**: Measures the average length of time between purchasing inventory on credit and making cash payment for that inventory. It helps assess how quickly a company pays its suppliers [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-55].

To calculate "Purchases" for the formula: `Purchases = COGS + Increase in Inventory` or `Purchases = COGS - Decrease in Inventory` [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-56].

#### Example: NTUC FairPrice (2021)
*   COGS: $3,040,635,000
*   Increase in Inventory: ($291,264,000 - $356,611,000) = ($65,347,000) --> *Correction: The slide shows "Increase in Inventory" as (32,673), which means it's likely (Ending - Beginning) inventory in thousands. Let's use slide values directly.* [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-56]
*   Purchases (COGS + Inc in Inv): $3,040,635,000 + ($32,673,000) = $3,007,962,000
*   Beginning Accounts Payable (2020): $703,485,000
*   Ending Accounts Payable (2021): $668,580,000
*   Average AP = ($703,485,000 + $668,580,000) / 2 = $686,033,000 [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-56].

*   **Number of Days' Purchases in AP**: 365 / ($3,007,962,000 / $686,033,000) = 365 / 4.384 = **83.25 days** [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-56].

#### NTUC FairPrice: Net Operating Cycle in 2021
*   Operating Cycle (time from purchase to cash collection): 42.72 days.
*   Days' Purchases in Accounts Payable (time until suppliers are paid): 83.25 days.

This means NTUC FairPrice receives cash from its customers (42.72 days) *before* it has to pay its suppliers (83.25 days). The difference represents "excess temporary capital" or **Negative Working Capital Cycle**, indicating that NTUC does not need to rely on external financing for cash to support its operating activities, as customer cash inflows cover supplier payments [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-57].

**Compare with Caterpillar's Net Operating Cycle**:
Caterpillar has to pay its suppliers in 65 days but waits 173 days before receiving cash from customers. This means Caterpillar needs to finance 108 days of its operating cycle through equity or debt financing [source: acc1701x-lecture-lecture-08-slides-inventory-pdf#section-58].
