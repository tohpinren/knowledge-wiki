# Lecture 09: Property, Plant & Equipment (PPE) & Intangible Assets

This page covers the accounting treatment of Property, Plant & Equipment (PPE) and Intangible Assets, which are crucial long-term operating assets for many businesses. It details their acquisition, various depreciation methods, the distinction between capitalizing and expensing additional expenditures, impairment, and disposal. The lecture also introduces intangible assets, their amortization, and key financial ratios for assessing asset efficiency.

### Long-term Operating Assets

Long-term operating assets are those held and used by a company to facilitate operating activities and generate revenues [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-4].

They are generally classified into two main types [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-4]:

1.  **Tangible Assets**: Assets with physical substance.
    *   **Property, Plant & Equipment (PPE)** (also known as "Fixed Assets"): Includes land, buildings, fixtures, and equipment.
    *   **Natural Resources**: Examples include mineral deposits, timber tracts, and oil fields.
2.  **Intangible Assets**: Assets with no physical substance, but which grant specific rights to their owners.
    *   **Definite Life Intangibles**: Assets that have a finite useful life, such as patents, copyrights, and franchises. These are subject to amortization.
    *   **Indefinite Life Intangibles**: Assets with an indefinite useful life, such as trademarks and goodwill. These are not amortized but are tested for impairment annually.

#### Real Financial Statements Examples
*   **Cathay Pacific Airlines (PPE)**: For an airline, PPE (e.g., aircraft, properties, and other equipment) typically constitutes a significant portion of its total assets. For Cathay Pacific in 2020, PPE represented 65% of its total assets, with aircraft and related equipment making up 58% [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-5].
*   **Pfizer (Intangibles)**: For pharmaceutical companies, intangible assets are often very substantial. In 2019, Pfizer's intangibles (identifiable intangible assets and goodwill) amounted to 56% of its total assets [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-5].

### Acquiring Property, Plant & Equipment (PPE)

PPE, being tangible assets actively used in operations for future benefits, are initially recorded at their **cost** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-6].

#### Acquisition Cost
The acquisition cost of PPE includes the purchase price and all expenditures necessary to prepare the asset for its intended use. It does *not* include financing charges [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-6].

Specific components of acquisition cost for different asset types [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-6]:
*   **Buildings**: Purchase/construction price, renovation and repair costs, brokerage fees, legal & title fees, taxes.
*   **Equipment**: Purchase price, installation costs, building modifications needed for installation, transportation costs & insurance, taxes.
*   **Land**: Purchase price, real estate commissions, title insurance premiums, delinquent taxes, surveying fees, title search & transfer fees, legal & escrow fees. *Land is **NOT** depreciable*.
*   **Land Improvements**: Improvement cost (including materials, labor & overhead). Examples: parking lots, driveways, fences, walks, shrubs, lighting systems, landscaping.

#### Recording Acquisition
Acquisition of PPE can be for cash or through debt [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-6]:
*   **Acquisition for Cash**: If Cathay Pacific purchases aircraft equipment for $75,000,000 cash, the journal entry would be:
    *   Debit Aircraft Equipment $75,000,000
    *   Credit Cash $75,000,000
*   **Acquisition for Debt**: If Cathay Pacific purchases aircraft equipment for $1,000,000 cash and a $74,000,000 note payable, the journal entry would be:
    *   Debit Aircraft Equipment $75,000,000
    *   Credit Notes Payable $74,000,000
    *   Credit Cash $1,000,000

#### Lump-Sum (Basket) Purchase
When multiple assets are purchased for a single combined cost (lump-sum or basket purchase), the total cost is separated and allocated to each asset component based on their **relative fair market values** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-6].

*Example*: Cathay Pacific pays $90,000 to acquire land, a building, and land improvements. If the appraised values are Land $30,000, Land Improvements $10,000, and Building $60,000 (total appraised value $100,000), the $90,000 cost would be allocated as follows [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-6]:
*   Land: ($30,000/$100,000) \* $90,000 = $27,000
*   Land Improvements: ($10,000/$100,000) \* $90,000 = $9,000
*   Building: ($60,000/$100,000) \* $90,000 = $54,000
*   Total Apportioned Cost: $90,000

### Depreciation

**Depreciation** is a cost allocation process that systematically and rationally matches the acquisition costs of operational assets with the periods benefited by their use, adhering to the matching principle [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7].

#### Recording Depreciation
Depreciation is recorded with the following journal entry [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7]:
*   Debit Depreciation Expense (an Income Statement item)
*   Credit Accumulated Depreciation (a Balance Sheet item, specifically a contra-asset account linked to the PPE asset account, tracking total depreciation to date).

The **Net Book Value** (also known as "carrying amount" or undepreciated cost) of an asset is calculated as: Acquisition Cost – Accumulated Depreciation [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7].

#### Required Values for Depreciation Calculation
To calculate depreciation expense, three values are required for each asset [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7]:
1.  **Acquisition Cost**: The initial cost of obtaining the asset.
2.  **Estimated Useful Life**: The period over which the asset is expected to be available for use by the entity.
3.  **Estimated Residual (Salvage) Value**: The recoverable amount by the company at the end of the asset's useful life.

#### Types of Depreciation Methods
There are three main types of depreciation methods [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7]:
1.  Straight-line method
2.  Units-of-production method
3.  Declining-balance method

### 1) Straight-Line Method

The straight-line method is the most common depreciation method. It recognizes an equal portion of depreciation expense over the asset's useful life [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7].

**Formula** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7]:
$$ \text{Annual Depreciation Expense} = \frac{\text{Cost} - \text{Residual Value}}{\text{Useful Life in Years}} $$

*Example*: Cathay purchased ground equipment for $62,500 with an estimated useful life of 3 years and a residual value of $2,500 [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7].
$$ \text{Depreciation Expense per Year} = \frac{\$62,500 - \$2,500}{3 \text{ years}} = \$20,000 \text{ per year} $$

#### Effect on Financial Statements (FS)
*   **Income Statement**: Depreciation expense is recognized equally each period for the length of the useful life [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7].
    *   Year 1: Depreciation expense $20,000
    *   Year 2: Depreciation expense $20,000
    *   Year 3: Depreciation expense $20,000
*   **Statement of Financial Position**: The Net Book Value (cost – accumulated depreciation) decreases over time [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7].
    *   Year 1: NBV = $62,500 – $20,000 = $42,500
    *   Year 2: NBV = $62,500 – $40,000 = $22,500
    *   Year 3: NBV = $62,500 – $60,000 = $2,500 (which equals the residual value)

A depreciation schedule visually tracks the depreciation expense, accumulated depreciation, and net book value of an asset over its useful life [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7].

### 2) Units-of-Production Method

This method allocates depreciable costs based on the total estimated productive output of an asset [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7].

#### Calculation Steps
1.  **Calculate the depreciation rate per unit** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7]:
    $$ \text{Depreciation Rate per Unit} = \frac{\text{Cost} - \text{Residual Value}}{\text{Total Units of Production}} $$
2.  **Calculate depreciation expense based on actual production** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7]:
    $$ \text{Depreciation Expense} = \text{Depreciation Rate per Unit} \times \text{Actual Units Produced} $$

*Example*: Cathay purchased ground equipment for $62,500 with a total useful life of 100,000 miles and a residual value of $2,500. In the first year, it was used for 30,000 miles [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7].
1.  Depreciation Rate = ($62,500 - $2,500) / 100,000 miles = $0.60 per mile
2.  Depreciation Expense (Year 1) = $0.60 per mile \* 30,000 miles = $18,000

#### Natural Resources: Depletion
Natural resources (e.g., mineral deposits, timber tracts, oil fields) have acquisition, exploration, and development costs. These costs are allocated over the periods benefited by means of **depletion**, which is conceptually similar to depreciation [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7]. Depletion expense is calculated using the units-of-production method and is reported as cost less accumulated depletion in the noncurrent assets portion of the balance sheet [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7].

### 3) Declining-Balance Method

The declining-balance method (also known as "accelerated depreciation") matches higher depreciation expense with higher revenues in the early years of an asset's useful life when it is typically more efficient. This means more depreciation is taken at the beginning and less at the end [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7].

This method applies a rate exceeding the straight-line rate (e.g., 2x or 1.5x) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7]. The **Double-Declining-Balance (DDB)** method applies a rate that is double (2x) the straight-line rate [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7].

#### Calculating Annual Depreciation
1.  **Calculate the straight-line rate** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7]:
    *   Example: For a useful life of 4 years, the straight-line rate = 100% / 4 = 25%.
2.  **Calculate the declining-balance rate (at 'x' times)** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7]:
    *   Example: For double (2x) declining, DDB rate = 2 \* 25% = 50%.
3.  **Calculate annual depreciation expense using the declining-balance rate** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7]:
    *   Depreciation Expense = Net Book Value (at the beginning of the period) \* Declining-Balance Rate.
    *   *Note that the residual value is ignored in the calculation itself, but the asset cannot be depreciated below its residual value* [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7].

**Quick Formula for Annual Depreciation** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7]:
$$ \text{Annual Depreciation Expense} = \text{Net Book Value} \times \left( \frac{\text{x}}{\text{Useful Life in Years}} \right) $$
Where 'x' is the declining balance multiplier (e.g., 2 for DDB).

*Example (Cathay Pacific)*: Equipment cost $62,500, useful life 3 years, residual value $2,500, using DDB [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7].
*   Straight-line rate = 1/3. DDB rate = 2/3.
*   **First year**: $62,500 \* (2/3) = $41,667
    *   NBV = $62,500 - $41,667 = $20,833
*   **Second year**: ($62,500 - $41,667) \* (2/3) = $20,833 \* (2/3) = $13,889
    *   NBV = $20,833 - $13,889 = $6,944
*   **Third year**: The calculated depreciation would be $6,944 \* (2/3) = $4,629. However, the book value cannot go below the residual value of $2,500. Therefore, depreciation expense is limited to the amount that reduces the book value to the residual value [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7].
    *   $6,944 (NBV at end of year 2) - $2,500 (Residual Value) = $4,444.
    *   So, Year 3 depreciation expense is $4,444.
    *   NBV = $6,944 - $4,444 = $2,500

#### Comparing Depreciation Methods
All three methods (Straight-Line, Units-of-Production, Double-Declining-Balance) start with the same cost and end with the same residual value, resulting in the same total depreciation over the asset's life. However, they yield different depreciation expenses in each period, which impacts reported profitability [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7].

#### Summary of Depreciation Methods
*   **Straight-line method**: Equal depreciation expense each year.
    *   `Depreciation Expense = (Cost - Residual Value) / Useful Life in Years` [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7]
*   **Units-of-Production method**: Varying amounts of depreciation expense each year based on production.
    *   `Depreciation Expense = ((Cost - Residual Value) / Life in Units of Production) * Actual Units Produced` [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7]
*   **Double-declining-balance (DDB) method**: Declining amount of depreciation expense over time (accelerated depreciation).
    *   `Depreciation Expense = Net Book Value * (2 / Useful Life in Years)` [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-7]

### Real Financial Statements: Cathay Pacific Depreciation Method

Cathay Pacific states that property, plant and equipment are recorded at cost less accumulated depreciation and impairment [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8]. The cost includes the purchase price and directly attributable costs to bring the asset to working condition. Depreciation of owned PPE is calculated on a **straight-line basis** over their anticipated useful lives to their estimated residual values [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

Examples of useful lives and residual values for Cathay Pacific [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8]:
*   Aircraft: 20-23 years to residual value of the lower of 1% of cost or expected realisable value.
*   Aircraft product: 5-10 years to nil residual value.
*   Other equipment: 3-25 years to nil residual value.
*   Buildings: Over the lease term of the leasehold land to nil residual value.

#### Reporting PPE on Statement of Financial Position
PPE is presented at its **net book value** on the Statement of Financial Position. For detailed information on acquisition cost and accumulated depreciation, users must refer to the notes to the financial statements [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

Cathay Pacific's Statement of Financial Position shows "Property, plant and equipment" as a non-current asset. Note 7 provides further breakdown [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

#### PPE Cost & Accumulated Depreciation (Cathay Pacific Note 7)
Note 7 in Cathay Pacific's financial statements details the cost, accumulated depreciation, and net book value of its PPE [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].
*   The "Cost" section shows additions, disposals, and transfers affecting the total cost of PPE.
*   The "Accumulated depreciation and impairment" section includes the "Charge for the year" which represents the total depreciation expense taken for the year. For example, in 2020, this was HK$13,848 million [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].
*   The "Net book value" at year-end represents the carrying amount of PPE.

#### Income Statement (Cathay Pacific)
Depreciation expense is typically included within "Operating expenses" on the income statement. For Cathay Pacific, "Aircraft depreciation and rentals" and "Other depreciation, amortisation and rentals" are listed. The total depreciation expense for the year (HK$13,848 million in 2020) is disclosed in the notes, such as Note 2, which breaks down operating profit [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

### Partial-Year Depreciation

When PPE is acquired during the year, depreciation is calculated only for the fraction of the year the asset was owned [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

*Example (Straight-line method)*: Equipment bought on August 1st, 2020, for $62,500, with a 3-year useful life and $2,500 residual value [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].
*   Annual Depreciation = ($62,500 - $2,500) / 3 years = $20,000
*   Depreciation Expense for 2020 (5 months: August to December) = $20,000 \* (5/12) = $8,334
*   Depreciation for 2021 and 2022 would be the full $20,000 each.
*   Depreciation for 2023 (remaining 7 months) = $20,000 \* (7/12) = $11,666
*   Total Depreciation = $8,334 + $20,000 + $20,000 + $11,666 = $60,000

### Changes in Depreciation Estimates

Depreciation relies on two estimates: estimated useful life and estimated residual value [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8]. If these estimates change, the asset is depreciated using the new residual value and/or over its remaining new useful life [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

*   Changes in estimates **do NOT** affect depreciation expense already taken.
*   Changes in estimates **only affect depreciation in future years** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

*Example (Cathay Pacific)*: Aircraft purchased for $60,000,000, depreciated straight-line over 20 years with a $3,000,000 residual value. Annual depreciation = ($60M - $3M) / 20 = $2.85M [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].
In Year 5, the estimated useful life is changed to 25 years (total) and the residual value is lowered to $2,400,000 [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].
*   Acquisition Cost: $60,000,000
*   Less: Accumulated Depreciation (Years 1-4): $2.85M \* 4 = ($11,400,000)
*   Remaining Book Value: $48,600,000
*   Less: New Residual Value: ($2,400,000)
*   New Revised Cost to Depreciate: $46,200,000
*   New Remaining Useful Life: 25 years (total) - 4 years (already passed) = 21 years
*   Revised Annual Depreciation (from Year 5 onwards): $46,200,000 / 21 years = $2,200,000

### Capitalize Versus Expense

Expenditures that provide future benefits can be capitalized (recorded as an asset) instead of expensed [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

#### Research & Development (R&D) Costs
*   **IFRS**: Research costs are expensed. Development costs can be capitalized once technological feasibility is established.
*   **GAAP**: Research & development costs are all expensed in the period incurred [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

#### Repairs and Additional Expenditures on PPE
Fixed assets require ongoing maintenance or improvements. Distinguishing whether to capitalize or expense these expenditures depends on their characteristics [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8]:

| Types of Expenditures            | Capitalize or Expense? | Identifying Characteristics                                                                                                               |
| :------------------------------- | :--------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1) Revenue Expenditures** (Ordinary repairs & maintenance) | Expense (Debit Expense)        | Maintains normal operating condition, does not increase productivity, does not extend life beyond original estimate, recurring, small amounts. |
| **2) Capital Expenditures** (Additions & improvements)    | Capitalize (Debit LT Asset)    | Major overhauls or partial replacements, usually infrequent, increases efficiency, extends useful life beyond original estimate, large amounts. |

#### Financial Statement Effect: Capitalize vs. Expense
*   **Expensing** (Revenue Expenditure):
    *   **Statement Affected**: Income Statement.
    *   **Effect on Net Income**: Lower Net Income (expense taken fully in current period).
*   **Capitalizing** (Capital Expenditure):
    *   **Statement Affected**: Balance Sheet.
    *   **Effect on Net Income**: Higher Net Income (expense taken slowly over useful lifetime through depreciation) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

Capitalizing expenditures creates incentives for companies to misclassify expenses to boost net income, as seen in the WorldCom scandal (2002), where $11 billion in expenditures were improperly capitalized [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8]. Many companies set a materiality threshold, expensing all expenditures below a certain amount [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

### Impairment of PPE

An **impairment** occurs when the carrying amount (net book value) of an asset exceeds its recoverable amount [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8]. This represents a loss of a portion of the utility and value of an asset due to factors like casualty, obsolescence, or lack of demand [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

*Example*: Equipment with a carrying amount of $8,000 ($9,000 cost less $1,000 accumulated depreciation) has a recoverable amount of $7,500 [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].
*   Impairment = Recoverable Amount – Carrying Amount = $7,500 - $8,000 = ($500) loss.
*   **Journal Entry**:
    *   Debit Impairment Loss on Equipment $500
    *   Credit Accumulated Impairment Loss $500

#### Real Financial Statements: Cathay Pacific Impairment
Cathay Pacific's Income Statement reports "Impairment and related charges" as part of its operating expenses [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8]. Note 32 provides details, indicating that in 2020, HK$4,056 million in pre-tax impairment and related charges were recognized. This included a HK$2,764 million reduction in asset values on 34 aircraft deemed unlikely to re-enter service [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8]. The accumulated depreciation and impairment table (Note 7) also shows impairment charges, for example, HK$2,355 million for owned aircraft and HK$409 million for finance leased aircraft, totaling HK$2,764 million [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

### Disposal of PPE

Companies may dispose of PPE before the end of its useful life through voluntary disposals (e.g., discard, sell, trade) or involuntary disposals (e.g., fire, accident) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

Disposal of PPE typically requires two journal entries [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8]:
1.  **An adjusting journal entry (AJE)** to update depreciation expense and accumulated depreciation accounts to the date of disposal.
2.  **An entry to record the disposal**:
    *   The cost of the asset and any accumulated depreciation related to the disposed asset are removed from the books.
    *   Any difference between cash received (for sale) and the asset's book value is recorded as either a **Gain** or **Loss** on disposal. Gains/losses from asset disposal are typically shown **after** Operating Income in the Income Statement, not as operating revenue/expense [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

#### Disposal by Sale (Cathay Pacific Example)
Cathay Pacific sold flight equipment for $11M cash at the end of its 17th year. The equipment originally cost $30M, had zero residual value, and a useful life of 25 years, depreciated via straight-line [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

1.  **Update Depreciation**:
    *   Annual Depreciation = ($30M - $0) / 25 Years = $1,200,000
    *   Journal Entry for 17th year depreciation:
        *   Debit Depreciation Expense $1,200,000
        *   Credit Accumulated Depreciation $1,200,000
2.  **Record Disposal**:
    *   Total Accumulated Depreciation = 17 years \* $1,200,000 = $20,400,000
    *   Asset Book Value = Cost $30,000,000 – Accumulated Depreciation $20,400,000 = $9,600,000
    *   Disposal resulted in a Gain = Cash received $11,000,000 - Asset book value $9,600,000 = $1,400,000
    *   **Journal Entry**:
        *   Debit Cash $11,000,000
        *   Debit Accumulated Depreciation $20,400,000
        *   Credit Aircraft Equipment $30,000,000
        *   Credit Gain on Sale of Asset $1,400,000

#### Disposal by Discarding (Cathay Pacific Example)
Flight equipment originally costing $30M, fully depreciated straight-line over 25 years with zero residual value, is discarded [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

*   **Remove Fully-Depreciated Asset**:
    *   Debit Accumulated Depreciation $30,000,000
    *   Credit Aircraft Equipment $30,000,000

If a discarded asset has not been fully depreciated, a loss is recognized. For example, if accumulated depreciation is $20M and there's a $50k disposal cost paid in cash [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8]:
*   Asset Book Value = $30,000,000 - $20,000,000 = $10,000,000
*   Loss on Disposal = $10,000,000 (NBV) + $50,000 (disposal cost) = $10,050,000
*   **Journal Entry**:
    *   Debit Accumulated Depreciation $20,000,000
    *   Debit Loss on Disposal of PPE $10,050,000
    *   Credit Aircraft Equipment $30,000,000
    *   Credit Cash $50,000

### Intangible Assets

Intangible assets are non-current assets without physical substance, often providing exclusive rights or privileges. Their acquisition cost (at purchase) is recorded at the current cash equivalent, including the purchase price, legal fees, and filing fees [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

#### Definite vs. Indefinite Life Intangibles
| Feature          | Definite Life Intangibles                                 | Indefinite Life Intangibles                                                                                                   |
| :--------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Amortization** | Amortized over its estimated useful life. Usually assumed to have no salvage value. Straight-line method is typically used. | Not amortized. Tested at least annually for possible impairment, and book value is reduced to fair value if impaired.           |
| **Examples**     | Patents, Copyrights, Franchises.                          | Trademarks, Goodwill.                                                                                                         |

**Amortization Expense** is recognized over the estimated useful life of definite-life intangibles [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].
*   **Journal Entry**:
    *   Debit Amortization Expense XX
    *   Credit Accumulated Amortization XX

#### Examples of Intangibles
*   **Goodwill**: Arises when one company buys another, representing the excess of the purchase price over the fair market value of acquired net assets. Only *purchased goodwill* is an intangible asset [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].
*   **Trademark**: An exclusive legal right to use a distinctive name, image, or slogan [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].
*   **Copyrights**: Exclusive rights to publish, use, and sell literary, musical, or artistic work [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].
*   **Technology**: Includes computer software (programs written by company employees) and web development [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].
*   **Patents**: Government-granted exclusive right to use, manufacture, and sell an invention [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].
*   **Franchise**: Contractual right to sell specific products or services, use trademarks, or perform activities in a region [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].
*   **Licenses & Operating Rights**: Permits owners to use public property for services, obtained from government units or agencies [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

#### "Intangibles" NOT Recorded in the Books
Intangibles that are *not acquired through an exchange* are generally not recorded in a company's books. This includes internally generated/developed brands, employee knowledge, or CEO's vision [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8]. Many companies have valuable internally generated brands that do not appear as assets on their balance sheets, leading to significant differences between market valuations and book values for brand-heavy companies (e.g., Apple, Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Facebook) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

#### Real Financial Statements: Cathay Pacific Intangibles
Intangibles are presented at their **net book value** on the Statement of Financial Position [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8]. Cathay Pacific's Note 8 provides details on "Intangible assets: Cost & Amortisation" [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8]. This note shows the cost, accumulated amortisation and impairment, and net book value for different types of intangibles like goodwill, computer software, and others. The "Charge for the year" line represents the amortisation expense for the period [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8]. For instance, in 2020, Cathay Pacific's amortisation expense was HK$573 million [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8]. This amount is also visible in Note 2, which details operating profit [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

### Financial Statement Analysis (FSA)

#### Fixed Assets Turnover Ratio
The Fixed Assets Turnover ratio measures how efficiently a company uses its fixed assets to generate sales [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8]. It provides a rough indication of how much sales are generated per dollar of PPE. It's also known as the "PPE Turnover ratio" [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

**Formula** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8]:
$$ \text{Fixed Assets Turnover} = \frac{\text{Net Sales}}{\text{Average Fixed Assets}} $$
Where `Average Fixed Assets = (Beginning Net PPE + Ending Net PPE) / 2` [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

#### Total Assets Turnover Ratio
The Total Assets Turnover ratio measures a company's overall ability to use its total assets to generate sales [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

**Formula** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8]:
$$ \text{Total Assets Turnover} = \frac{\text{Net Sales}}{\text{Average Total Assets}} $$
Where `Average Total Assets = (Beginning Total Assets + Ending Total Assets) / 2` [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].

#### Major Airlines Example (FY2019 & FY2020)
Comparing fixed assets and total assets turnover ratios across major international airlines reveals variations in efficiency. For example, in FY2019, Virgin Atlantic had a Fixed Assets Turnover of 1.411 and a Total Assets Turnover of 0.940, while Cathay Pacific had 0.832 and 0.529, respectively. These ratios can change significantly year-over-year due to factors like global events (e.g., the decline in turnover ratios for many airlines in FY2020 compared to FY2019) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-09-ppe-pdf#section-8].
