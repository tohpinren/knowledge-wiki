## Capital Structure: Debt vs. Equity

The acquisition of assets by a company is financed from two primary sources: Debt and Equity [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-6]. The specific mix of debt and equity used by a company is known as its "capital structure" [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-6].

### Difference Between Debt Financing & Equity Financing

| Feature            | Loan (Debt Financing)                                       | Investment (Equity Financing)                                              |
| :----------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Repayment**      | Company is **obligated to repay** the principal amount, even during bad times [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-7]. | Company is **not obligated to repay** the principal amount (investors can lose all of their investment) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-7]. |
| **Sharing Success** | Lender does not share in the success of the company (only repays borrowed amount) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-7]. | Investor shares in the success of the company (through dividends and capital gain from stock price appreciation) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-7]. |

Equity financing is generally considered riskier for the investor due to the lack of obligation for repayment, while debt financing carries an obligation for repayment.

## Recap: Types of Businesses

Businesses can typically be set up as:
1.  **Sole Proprietorship**: Owned by a single individual, not a legally separate entity, and the owner has unlimited liability. They are usually small and common in service, retail, and farming [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-8].
2.  **Partnership**: Owned by two or more individuals, not a legally separate entity from its owners [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-8].
3.  **Corporation**: This is the focus of this lecture [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-8].
    *   A corporation is a **legal entity separate from its owners**. It acts as an "artificial legal person" with rights similar to a human being, able to own property, pay taxes, exist indefinitely, sue, and be sued [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-8].
    *   Ownership is represented by **shares of stock**, which can be privately or publicly owned [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-8].

### Advantages and Disadvantages of Corporations

Corporations offer several advantages but also come with disadvantages [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-9]:

**Advantages**:
*   **Separate legal entity**: Distinct from its owners [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-9].
*   **Limited liability for stockholders**: Stockholders are only liable up to their investment share, not personally liable for corporate acts or obligations [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-9].
*   **Continuity of life**: The corporation exists independently and can "live on" indefinitely [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-9].
*   **Ease in transferring ownership**: Stock can be easily bought and sold on a stock exchange [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-9].
*   **Opportunities to raise large amounts of money**: By selling shares to a broad public [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-9].

**Disadvantages**:
*   **Government regulation**: Corporations are subject to more stringent government regulations [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-9].
*   **Double taxation**: Income is taxed twice—once at the corporate level when earned, and again when distributed to stockholders as dividends. (Note: Some countries like Singapore and Hong Kong have a one-tier tax system, meaning dividends are not taxed, making them attractive for businesses) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-9].

## Corporate Structure

The corporate structure typically follows a hierarchical model [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-10]:
1.  **Shareholders**: Owners of the corporation, at the top of the pyramid.
2.  **Board of Directors**: Elected by shareholders to oversee management.
3.  **President, Vice-President, and Other Officers**: Responsible for daily operations.
4.  **Employees of the Corporation**: Carry out the work.

### Shareholders' Rights

Shareholders (or stockholders) are individuals who invest in a corporation and possess specific rights [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-11]:
1.  **Voting rights**: To elect the Board of Directors and approve business matters, either in person or by proxy at stockholders' meetings [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-11].
2.  **Purchase additional shares / Sell shares**: Shareholders can sell their ownership for profit (capital gain) when stock prices rise, or purchase more shares [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-11].
3.  **Receive dividends**: A proportionate share of the distributions of profits (dividends rights) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-11].
4.  **Residual Claim**: A proportionate share of the distributions of remaining assets upon liquidation of the company. Creditors are paid before shareholders in the event of liquidation or bankruptcy (corporate assets rights) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-11].

## Ownership of a Corporation: Types of Shares

Understanding the different categories of shares is crucial for comprehending corporate ownership [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-12]:
*   **Authorized shares**: The maximum number of shares of capital stock that a corporation is legally permitted to sell to the public, as stated in its corporate charter [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-12].
*   **Issued shares**: Authorized shares of stock that have been sold [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-12].
*   **Unissued shares**: Authorized shares of stock that have never been sold [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-12].
*   **Outstanding shares**: Issued shares that are currently owned by stockholders [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-12].
*   **Treasury shares**: Issued shares that have been reacquired by the corporation [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-12].

### Par Value vs. No Par Value

The concept of par value is important for accounting for shares [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-13]:
*   **Par Value**: An arbitrary (nominal) amount assigned to each share of stock when it is authorized. When par-value stock sells for above par, it is said to sell at a premium. Par value is **not** related to the market value (selling price) of the stock [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-13].
*   **No Par Value**: No arbitrary amount is assigned to each share of stock. A "stated" value might be assigned instead to help calculate amounts recorded upon share issuance [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-13].

**In Singapore**, companies typically use **NO PAR value shares**, and there is no authorized share capital requirement [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-13].

### Major Types of Shares

Corporations primarily issue two types of shares: Ordinary and Preferred [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-14, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-15].

#### (1) Ordinary Shares
Ordinary shares (also known as common stock) are the basic voting stock [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-14]:
*   Typically, one share equals one vote, although dual-class shares with different voting rights can exist [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-14].
*   Holders are entitled to receive declared dividends [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-14].
*   They rank behind preference shares in terms of dividends and usually in liquidation [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-14].
*   May have **preemptive rights**, allowing existing stockholders to purchase additional shares to maintain their percentage of ownership [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-14].

#### (2) Preferred Shares
Preferred shares often have specific features that differentiate them from ordinary shares [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-15]:
*   **No voting rights** is common [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-15].
*   **Priority in payment**: Often have fixed-rate dividends that must be paid before ordinary shareholders receive any dividends [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-15].
*   Usually rank before ordinary shares in both dividends and liquidation [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-15].
*   **Classifications of preferred shares**:
    *   **Convertible / Non-convertible**: Convertible shares can be exchanged for ordinary shares [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-15].
    *   **Redeemable / Non-redeemable**: Redeemable shares give the company an option to "buyback" the shares [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-15].
    *   **Cumulative / Non-cumulative**: Cumulative shares require all dividends in arrears (outstanding unpaid dividends from past years) to be fully paid before any ordinary dividends can be distributed [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-15].
    *   **Participating / Non-participating**: Participating shares may receive additional dividends based on predetermined conditions [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-15].

### Reasons for Issuing Preference Shares
Companies might issue preference shares for several strategic reasons [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-19]:
*   To raise capital without sacrificing control.
*   To boost the return earned by ordinary shareholders through financial leverage.
*   To appeal to investors who may consider ordinary shares too risky or their expected return too low.

### Issue of Ordinary Shares (with par value)

When ordinary shares with a par value are issued, the accounting entries separate the par value from any amount received in excess of par [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-16].

**(1) Issuance for cash at premium** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-16]:
If StartMeUp Company issues 1,000 ordinary shares of $1 par-value for $5 per share, fully paid and received in cash:

| Account                          | Debit     | Credit    |
| :------------------------------- | :-------- | :-------- |
| Cash ($1,000 x $5)               | $5,000    |           |
| Common Stock ($1,000 x $1)       |           | $1,000    |
| Paid-in Capital in Excess of Par |           | $4,000    |

**(2) Issuance for Acquisition of Asset (based on market value of shares)** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-16]:
If StartMeUp issues 5,000 ordinary shares of $1 par-value to acquire land, and the fair market value of the land cannot be determined, but the market value of the shares on the transaction date is $7 per share:

| Account                          | Debit     | Credit    |
| :------------------------------- | :-------- | :-------- |
| Land ($5,000 x $7)               | $35,000   |           |
| Common Stock ($5,000 x $1)       |           | $5,000    |
| Paid-in Capital in Excess of Par |           | $30,000   |

### Issue of Ordinary Shares (No par)

For no-par value shares, the entire proceeds are typically recorded as Common Stock [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-17].

**(1) Issuance for Cash** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-17]:
If StartMeUp Company issues 10,000 no par value ordinary shares for $20,000 cash:

| Account                      | Debit     | Credit    |
| :--------------------------- | :-------- | :-------- |
| Cash                         | $20,000   |           |
| Common Stock – ordinary no par shares |           | $20,000   |

**(2) Issuance for Acquisition of Asset (based on market value of asset)** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-17]:
If StartMeUp issues 5,000 no par value ordinary shares to purchase equipment with a fair value of $50,000, where the share price on the date of transaction is $7 per share:

| Account                      | Debit     | Credit    |
| :--------------------------- | :-------- | :-------- |
| Equipment                    | $50,000   |           |
| Common Stock – ordinary no par shares |           | $50,000   |
(Note: If the fair market value of the asset can be determined, that value is used as the basis for recording the transaction) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-17].

**(3) Issuance of stated value shares (No par with stated value)** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-18]:
If StartMeUp Company issues 10,000 no par value ordinary shares with a stated value of $4 for $5 per share:

| Account                       | Debit     | Credit    |
| :---------------------------- | :-------- | :-------- |
| Cash                          | $50,000   |           |
| Common Stock – ordinary no par shares |           | $40,000   |
| Common Stock Premium – ordinary |           | $10,000   |

**Terminology Note**: "Common Stock" can also be referred to as "Share Capital" or "Common Share." "Common Stock Premium" or "Paid-in Capital in excess of par" can also be referred to as "Share Premium" [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-18].

### Issue of Preferred Shares

Journal entries for issuing preferred shares depend on whether they have a par value or are no-par [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-20].

**No par preferred shares** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-20]:
If UOB issued 3,000 Class E non-cumulative non-convertible preference shares, with total proceeds of $330,000:

| Account                     | Debit     | Credit    |
| :-------------------------- | :-------- | :-------- |
| Cash                        | $330,000  |           |
| Preferred Stock – Class E   |           | $330,000  |

**Par value preferred shares** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-20]:
If UOB issued 3,000 Class E preferred shares with a par value of $100, for cash at $110 per share:

| Account                               | Debit     | Credit    |
| :------------------------------------ | :-------- | :-------- |
| Cash ($3,000 x $110)                  | $330,000  |           |
| Preferred Stock – Class E ($3,000 x $100) |           | $300,000  |
| Paid-in Capital in Excess of par, preferred |           | $30,000   |

(Note: Different classes of shares have different shareholder rights, often named with alphabets like A, B, C) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-20].

### Real Financial Statement Examples

*   **United Airlines (par value shares)**: Shows common stock at par, additional capital invested, stock held in treasury, retained earnings, and accumulated other comprehensive loss. It has authorized and par value shares, with no preferred shares but common and treasury shares [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-21].
*   **Cathay Pacific (no par value shares)**: Presents share capital, reserves, and funds attributable to shareholders. It utilizes no par value shares, with both ordinary and preference share types [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-22].
*   **Singapore Airlines (no par value shares)**: Displays share capital, mandatory convertible bonds, treasury shares, and other reserves. It uses no par value shares, comprising ordinary, mandatory convertible bonds, and treasury shares [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-23].

## Share Issuance in Public Markets: IPO & SEO

Shares are issued to the public through distinct processes [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-24]:
1.  **Initial Public Offering (IPO)**: This is the first time a corporation sells shares to the public [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-24].
2.  **Secondary/Seasoned Equity Offering (SEO)**: These are subsequent sales of new shares to the public after the initial IPO [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-24].

Once the initial sale of shares from the company to investors is complete, investors can then trade these shares among themselves in **secondary markets** (e.g., New York Stock Exchange NYSE, Hong Kong Stock Exchange HKSE, Singapore Exchange SGX) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-24]. Importantly, transactions between two investors in the secondary markets **do NOT affect the corporation's accounting records** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-24].

## Stock Repurchases: Treasury Shares (Treasury Stock)

Companies sometimes **buy back their own shares** from existing shareholders for various reasons [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-27]:
*   To use shares to acquire other companies.
*   To prevent a hostile takeover.
*   To reissue them to employees as compensation.
*   To signal management's confidence in the current stock price, thereby stimulating trading.
*   To return cash to shareholders.
*   To increase reported Earnings Per Share (EPS) by reducing the number of outstanding shares.

Repurchased shares are called **Treasury Shares** or **Treasury Stock** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-27].
*   A corporation records treasury shares **at cost** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-27].
*   Treasury shares have **no voting or dividend rights** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-27].
*   Treasury shares are a **contra-equity account**, meaning they reduce total shareholders' equity [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-27, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-29, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-30].

**Example (Berkshire Hathaway)**: If Berkshire Hathaway repurchased 20,000 shares at $400,000 per share, the total cost would be $8 billion. The journal entry would be [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-29]:

| Account         | Debit       | Credit      |
| :-------------- | :---------- | :---------- |
| Treasury Shares | $8 billion  |             |
| Cash            |             | $8 billion  |

### Singapore Rules on Share Buybacks (SGX)

Companies listed on the Singapore Exchange (SGX) must adhere to specific rules for share buybacks [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-28]:
*   **Shareholders' approval** is required [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-28].
*   Buybacks should **not occur during material developments** or when there's unannounced material information [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-28].
*   Companies must **avoid creating a "false market"** by [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-28]:
    *   Avoiding buying back shares near or at the close of the market.
    *   Buying back at relatively low prices (no more than 105% of the average closing market prices over the last 5 consecutive active trading days).
    *   Ensuring buybacks are not excessive (more than 30% of the daily volume of shares traded).

### Reissue of Treasury Shares

A company can reissue (sell) its treasury shares back to investors. The accounting treatment varies depending on the reissuance price relative to the acquisition cost [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-31].

**(a) Reissue at cost** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-31]:
Reissue 5,000 treasury shares at $400,000 per share (same price as repurchase):

| Account         | Debit       | Credit      |
| :-------------- | :---------- | :---------- |
| Cash            | $2 billion  |             |
| Treasury Shares |             | $2 billion  |

**(b) Reissue higher than cost** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-31]:
Reissue 10,000 treasury shares at $450,000 per share (higher than repurchase price of $400,000):

| Account                       | Debit       | Credit      |
| :---------------------------- | :---------- | :---------- |
| Cash (10,000 x $450k)         | $4.5 billion |             |
| Treasury Shares (10,000 x $400k) |             | $4 billion  |
| Premium on Treasury Shares*   |             | $0.5 billion |
(*Can also be labeled as "Paid-in Capital, Treasury Stock" or be part of the Equity Account) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-31].

**(c) Reissue lower than cost (sufficient balance in treasury share premium account)** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-32]:
Reissue 5,000 treasury shares at $350,000 per share (lower than repurchase price of $400,000), assuming a $0.5 billion premium balance from previous reissues:

| Account                         | Debit       | Credit      |
| :------------------------------ | :---------- | :---------- |
| Cash (5,000 x $350k)            | $1.75 billion |             |
| Premium on Treasury Shares ($0.5b - $0.25b used) | $0.25 billion |             |
| Treasury Shares (5,000 x $400k) |             | $2 billion  |

**(d) Reissue lower than cost (insufficient balance in treasury share premium account)** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-32]:
Reissue 5,000 treasury shares at $300,000 per share (lower than repurchase price of $400,000), assuming only a $0.25 billion premium balance remains:

| Account                         | Debit       | Credit      |
| :------------------------------ | :---------- | :---------- |
| Cash (5,000 x $300k)            | $1.5 billion |             |
| Premium on Treasury Shares (used fully) | $0.25 billion |             |
| Retained Earnings ($2b - $1.5b - $0.25b) | $0.25 billion |             |
| Treasury Shares (5,000 x $400k) |             | $2 billion  |
(Note: When the premium account does not have sufficient balance, Retained Earnings will be reduced) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-32].

## Retained Earnings (RE)

**Retained Earnings (RE)** represent the portion of a corporation's equity that has been earned from profitable operations and has not been distributed to stockholders [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-33].
*   **Increases in RE**: Primarily due to Net Income [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-33].
*   **Decreases in RE**: Due to Net Loss, dividend distributions, and some treasury stock transactions [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-33].
*   It's important to note that **RE is NOT cash**; it represents an accumulated profit figure within equity, not a cash balance [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-33].

## Distribution of Ordinary Dividends

Companies can distribute profits to shareholders through dividends, which are not legally required and are at the company's discretion [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-34].

### Types of Dividends
1.  **Cash dividend**: Paid in cash to stockholders [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-34].
2.  **Stock dividend**: Paid in the form of additional stock to stockholders [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-34].

To declare and pay dividends, a company must have [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-34]:
1.  Sufficient balance in retained earnings.
2.  Sufficient cash to pay the dividends (for cash dividends).

### Important Dates for Cash Dividends
The distribution of cash dividends involves three key dates [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-35]:

1.  **Declaration date**: The Board of Directors declares the dividend, and the company records a liability.
    *   **Journal Entry**:
        | Account                   | Debit | Credit |
        | :------------------------ | :---- | :----- |
        | Dividends – Ordinary Shares | XX    |        |
        | Dividends Payables        |       | XX     |

2.  **Date of record**: Stockholders holding shares on this date will receive the dividend. **No accounting entry** is made on this date [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-35].

3.  **Date of payment**: The company pays the dividends.
    *   **Journal Entry**:
        | Account            | Debit | Credit |
        | :----------------- | :---- | :----- |
        | Dividends Payables | XX    |        |
        | Cash               |       | XX     |

At the end of the accounting period, "Dividends" is a temporary account and must be closed out to Retained Earnings [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-35]:

*   **Closing Entry**:
    | Account          | Debit | Credit |
    | :--------------- | :---- | :----- |
    | Retained Earnings | XX    |        |
    | Dividends        |       | XX     |

### Distribution of Preferred Dividends

Preferred stock usually has a dividend percentage associated with it, and preferred dividends are paid based on this percentage [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-38].

*   **Current-Dividend Preference**: Preferred stockholders have the right to receive current dividends before common stockholders [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-38].
*   **Cumulative-Dividend Preference**: Requires that preferred stockholders be paid any dividends in arrears (unpaid dividends from past years) and current dividends before common stockholders receive any dividends [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-38].
    *   Dividends in arrears are unpaid dividends from past years [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-38].
    *   Dividends in arrears do not represent actual liabilities and are thus not recorded in the accounts; instead, they are reported in the notes to the financial statements [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-38].

**Example: Preferred & Ordinary Dividends** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-39]
Kites, Inc. declared $50,000 in dividends this year.
*   Ordinary shares: 100,000 shares, no par value.
*   Cumulative Preferred shares: 3%, $100 liquidation preference, 5,000 shares. (Dividends not paid last year).
*   Noncumulative Preferred shares: 6%, $50 liquidation preference, 3,000 shares.

Distribution breakdown:

| Dividend Allocation               | Calculation                                   | Amount      |
| :-------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------- | :---------- |
| **Total Dividends Declared**      |                                               | **$50,000** |
| **Cumulative Preferred Shares**   |                                               |             |
| Dividend in arrears (last year)   | [3% x 5,000 shares x $100]                    | $15,000     |
| Current year dividend             | [3% x 5,000 shares x $100]                    | $15,000     |
| **Subtotal for Cumulative Preferred** |                                               | **$30,000** |
| Remainder (before Noncumulative)  | ($50,000 - $30,000)                           | $20,000     |
| **Noncumulative Preferred Shares** |                                               |             |
| Current year dividend             | [6% x 3,000 shares x $50]                     | $9,000      |
| Remainder (before Ordinary)       | ($20,000 - $9,000)                            | $11,000     |
| **Ordinary Shares**               |                                               |             |
| Current year dividend             |                                               | $11,000     |
| Current year dividend per share   | [$11,000 / 100,000 shares]                    | $0.11       |

### Distribution of Stock Dividends

**Stock dividends** are a form of dividend where a company issues new shares to its shareholders instead of cash [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-40]. The accounting treatment depends on whether it's a "small" or "large" stock dividend. The dividing line is often set at 20-25% of the company's issued shares [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-40].

*   **Small stock dividends**: Assigned the **fair value** of the shares [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-40].
*   **Large stock dividends**: Assigned the **par value** of the shares [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-40].

**Example: Small Stock Dividends** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-41]
Macadamia & Co declares a 10% stock dividend (10,000 new shares). Common stock has a par value of $1 and market value of $7.

*   **@ Declaration date**:
    | Account                          | Debit     | Credit    |
    | :------------------------------- | :-------- | :-------- |
    | Stock Dividends (10,000 x $7)    | $70,000   |           |
    | Stock Dividends Distributable    |           | $10,000   |
    | Paid-in Capital in Excess of Par |           | $60,000   |

*   **@ Distribution date**:
    | Account                       | Debit     | Credit    |
    | :---------------------------- | :-------- | :-------- |
    | Stock Dividends Distributable | $10,000   |           |
    | Common Stock                  |           | $10,000   |

*   **@ Closing**:
    | Account         | Debit     | Credit    |
    | :-------------- | :-------- | :-------- |
    | Retained Earnings | $70,000   |           |
    | Stock Dividends |           | $70,000   |

**Example: Large Stock Dividends** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-42]
Macadamia & Co declares a 40% stock dividend (40,000 new shares). Common stock has a par value of $1 and market value of $7.

*   **@ Declaration date**:
    | Account                       | Debit     | Credit    |
    | :---------------------------- | :-------- | :-------- |
    | Stock Dividends (40,000 x $1) | $40,000   |           |
    | Stock Dividends Distributable |           | $40,000   |

*   **@ Distribution date**:
    | Account                       | Debit     | Credit    |
    | :---------------------------- | :-------- | :-------- |
    | Stock Dividends Distributable | $40,000   |           |
    | Common Stock                  |           | $40,000   |

*   **@ Closing**:
    | Account         | Debit     | Credit    |
    | :-------------- | :-------- | :-------- |
    | Retained Earnings | $40,000   |           |
    | Stock Dividends |           | $40,000   |

### Stock Split vs. Stock Dividend

Both stock splits and stock dividends increase the number of shares outstanding, but they differ in their accounting impact [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-43].

A **stock split** increases the number of shares outstanding in the same proportion that the par or stated value per share decreases, such that **total equity remains unchanged** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-43]. For example, a 2-for-1 stock split means 1,000 shares of $1 par become 2,000 shares of $0.5 par. The market value per share also reduces proportionately [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-43].

**Comparison**:

| Items to be Affected               | Stock Split | Stock Dividends (Small/Large) |
| :--------------------------------- | :---------- | :---------------------------- |
| Number of shares outstanding       | Increase    | Increase                      |
| Total equity                       | No change   | No change                     |
| Retained earnings                  | No change   | Decrease                      |
| Common stock                       | No change   | Increase                      |
| Paid-in capital in excess of par   | No change   | Increase (if any)             |
| Par value per share                | Decrease    | No change                     |
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-43]

## Other Comprehensive Income (OCI) & Reserves

**Other Comprehensive Income (OCI)** represents items of income and expense that are **not recognized in the profit or loss statement** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-44]. These items are accumulated in an equity account called "**Reserves**" or as "Other Equity" [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-44].

Examples of OCI items include [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-44]:
*   Exchange/currency differences when translating foreign operations.
*   Fair value revaluations.
*   Equity investments designated at fair value.
*   Gain/loss not recognized in profit or loss statements, such as hedging instruments and changes in revaluation surplus.

## Financial Statement Analysis (FSA)

### Earnings Per Share (EPS)

**Earnings Per Share (EPS)** measures a company's profitability by indicating its ability to produce income for each ordinary share outstanding [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-48]. It is one of the most widely watched financial ratios, as per-share numbers make comparisons between companies easier [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-48].

**Formula**:
$\text{Basic Earnings Per Share} = \frac{\text{Net Profit – Preferred Dividends}}{\text{Weighted-average Ordinary Shares Outstanding}}$ [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-48]

EPS is a required disclosure on the Income Statement. Companies must also disclose **diluted EPS**, which assumes all convertible securities (e.g., convertible preferred stock, convertible bonds, stock options) are converted into ordinary shares, thus potentially lowering EPS [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-48].

### Price-Earnings (PE) Ratio

The **Price-Earnings (PE) Ratio** assesses market expectations by measuring what the market is willing to pay for a company's current earnings stream [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-51].

**Formula**:
$\text{Price-Earnings Ratio} = \frac{\text{Market value (price) per share}}{\text{EPS}}$ [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-51]

A very high PE ratio typically indicates that a company is overpriced, suggesting high market expectations for future growth [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-51]. Real-world examples show varying PE ratios:
*   **Berkshire Hathaway**: A well-established blue-chip company, typically exhibits more stable and lower PE ratios [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-52].
*   **Amazon**: Its PE ratio stabilized around 70-90x in recent years, but historically reached peaks like 3,732x in 2012, reflecting high growth expectations [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-52].
*   **Tesla**: Showed an exceptionally high PE ratio of 1,274x at the end of 2020. This reflected significant investor confidence despite not achieving profitability until 2020. By October 2022, after multiple stock splits and substantial net income growth, its PE ratio was around 80x [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-53].

### Dividend Payout Ratio

The **Dividend Payout Ratio** measures the percentage of net income that a company distributes as cash dividends during the year [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-54].

**Formula**:
$\text{Dividend Payout Ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash Dividends}}{\text{Net Income}}$ [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-54]

*   Companies that consistently pay large dividends are referred to as **income shares** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-54].
*   Companies that distribute little or no dividends are typically called **growth shares**, as they tend to reinvest profits back into the business for expansion [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-54].

**Example**: Berkshire Hathaway, under Warren Buffett, famously has a Dividend Payout Ratio of **ZERO**. Warren Buffett believes profits should be reinvested into the company, used to acquire other valuable companies, or for stock repurchases, rather than distributed as dividends [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-10-slides-equity-pdf#section-55].
