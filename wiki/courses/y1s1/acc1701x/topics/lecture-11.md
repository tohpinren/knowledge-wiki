## Introduction to the Statement of Cash Flows (SCF)

The Statement of Cash Flows (SCF) explains how cash, as reported on the Statement of Financial Position, changes from the beginning to the end of a period. It answers critical questions: "Where did the cash come from?", "Where did the cash go?", and "What explains the change in cash balance?" [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-9].

**Cash** includes currency and cash equivalents. **Cash equivalents** are short-term, highly liquid investments that are readily convertible into cash, typically with maturity dates of less than three months, and carry little risk of value change due to interest rates [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-9].

### Importance of Cash Flow

"Cash is King!" Cash flow represents a company's ongoing ability to generate and use cash [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-5]. Positive cash flow is vital for a company's success because it:
*   Drives daily operations and increases purchasing power.
*   Allows for expansion and new investment opportunities.
*   Provides greater protection against creditors.
*   Gives greater flexibility to respond to critical situations.
*   Enables payment of dividends to owners [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-5].

Conversely, without sufficient cash, even companies with high net income can face financial distress and bankruptcy [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-6]. The case of Hyflux, which experienced volatile and negative operating cash flows despite auditors giving a "clean opinion," illustrates how a company can face financial distress and collapse when cash runs out [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-7, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-8].

## Classification of Cash Flows

The SCF classifies cash inflows and outflows into three major activities [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-13]:
1.  Operating Activities (CFO)
2.  Investing Activities (CFI)
3.  Financing Activities (CFF)

The typical sequencing of activities on the SCF is Operating, Investing, and Financing [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-4].

### 1. Cash Flows from Operating Activities (CFO)

CFO includes cash inflows and outflows directly related to a company's normal business operations—the principal revenue-producing activities that typically determine net income [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-13, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-14].

*   **Inflows:**
    *   Cash received from customers (from sales of goods or services).
    *   Cash received from royalties, fees, commissions, and other revenue [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-14].
*   **Outflows:**
    *   Cash paid for purchases of goods and services from suppliers.
    *   Cash paid for salaries and wages.
    *   Cash paid for income taxes.
    *   Cash paid for other operating expenses (e.g., rent, utilities) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-14].

### 2. Cash Flows from Investing Activities (CFI)

CFI encompasses cash inflows and outflows related to the acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and other investments not included in cash equivalents [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-13, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-15].

*   **Inflows:**
    *   Cash received from the sale or disposal of Property, Plant & Equipment (PPE) and other long-term assets.
    *   Cash received from the sale or maturity of investments in securities.
    *   Cash received from the repayment of loans made to other parties [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-15].
*   **Outflows:**
    *   Cash paid for the purchase of PPE and other long-term assets.
    *   Cash paid for the purchase of investments in securities.
    *   Cash paid for loans made to other parties [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-15].

An example of a cash flow from investing activities would be cash receipts from the sale of equipment. "Collection of a long-term note receivable" and "Sale of a building" would also be investing activities. "Amounts borrowed" would not be an investing activity [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-5].

### 3. Cash Flows from Financing Activities (CFF)

CFF involves cash inflows and outflows related to external sources of financing, specifically from owners and creditors [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-13, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-16].

*   **Inflows:**
    *   Cash received from borrowings (on loans, notes, bonds, etc.).
    *   Cash received from issuing shares to owners [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-16].
*   **Outflows:**
    *   Cash paid for the repayment of principal to creditors.
    *   Cash paid for repurchasing shares from shareholders.
    *   Cash paid for dividends to owners [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-16].

An example of a cash flow from financing activities would be cash receipts from the issuance of long-term debt. Cash receipts from interest on notes receivable and dividends on long-term investments are operating activities, while cash receipts from the sale of equipment are investing activities [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-4, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-5].

### Classification of Interest & Dividends

International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) allow entities to choose how to classify interest and dividends in their SCF, provided they are consistent. In this module, it is assumed that **all interest and dividends are treated as operating cash flows, except for dividends paid, which are part of financing cash flow** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-17].

### Non-Cash Investing & Financing Activities

Some investing and financing transactions do not involve cash and thus will not appear directly in the SCF. These non-cash transactions must be disclosed separately in the notes to the financial statements [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-18]. Examples include:
*   Retirement of debt by issuing equity securities.
*   Conversion of preference shares to ordinary shares.
*   Acquisition of long-term assets by issuing equity shares [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-18].

### Sample Format of SCF

A standard SCF format includes sections for operating, investing, and financing activities. Each section lists individual inflows and outflows, resulting in net cash provided (or used) by that activity. These net amounts are summed to calculate the net increase (or decrease) in cash for the period. This net change is then added to the beginning cash balance to arrive at the ending cash balance, which must reconcile with the cash and cash equivalents reported on the Statement of Financial Position [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-19, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-47]. Separate disclosure of non-cash investing and financing transactions is also required [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-19].

## SCF Relationships to the Balance Sheet (BS) and Income Statement (IS)

While Balance Sheets and Income Statements are prepared using journal entries and T-accounts based on accrual accounting, the Statement of Cash Flows cannot be directly prepared using T-accounts in the same way. Instead, numbers from the BS and IS (which are accrual-based) must be analyzed and adjusted to a cash basis to prepare the SCF [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-20].

Information needed to prepare the SCF includes:
*   Comparative Balance Sheets (or Statements of Financial Positions).
*   Current Income Statement.
*   Additional details concerning selected accounts [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-20].

## Cash Flow from Operations (CFO) - Indirect Method

There are two acceptable formats for presenting CFO: the Indirect Method and the Direct Method. This module focuses exclusively on the **Indirect Method** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-21].

The Indirect Method starts with accrual-basis Profit Before Tax (PBT) and adjusts it for non-cash transactions, deferrals, accruals, and items associated with investing or financing cash flows to convert it to cash-basis CFO [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-21, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-22, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-23]. This is the most commonly used method [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-21].

The overall process involves four categories of adjustments to Profit Before Tax (PBT):
A.  Operating items not generating or using cash (e.g., depreciation expense).
B.  Changes in non-cash current assets and current liabilities.
C.  Non-operating items.
D.  Separate reporting of dividend received, interest received/paid, and income taxes [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-22].

### Intuition Behind CFO Adjustments (Indirect Method)

The adjustments aim to convert accrual-basis Profit Before Tax (PBT) into cash-basis Cash from Operations (CFO) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-1].

1.  **Depreciation & Amortization:** These are non-cash expenses, so they are **added back** to PBT because no cash was involved in these expenses [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-23].

2.  **Noncash Current Assets & Current Liabilities:**
    *   **Increase in Noncash Current Assets:** **Deduct** from PBT. This represents either revenues included in PBT for which no cash was received (e.g., Accounts Receivable) or the use of cash for items not yet expensed (e.g., Inventory) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-23, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-24].
        *   **Example: Increase in Accounts Receivable (AR)**: An increase in AR from sales is a non-cash transaction. While included in PBT under accrual accounting, no cash was received. Therefore, to convert PBT to a cash basis, this amount is **deducted** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-1].
        *   **Example: Increase in Inventories**: If inventory increased through a cash purchase but was not sold (and thus not recorded as Cost of Goods Sold in PBT), the cash payment must be accounted for. Thus, this amount is **deducted** from PBT [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-2].
    *   **Decrease in Noncash Current Assets:** **Add** to PBT. This represents cash received from credit customers not yet reflected in net income (e.g., decrease in AR means cash collected) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-23, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-24, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-7].
        *   **Example: Decrease in Prepaid Expenses**: A decrease in prepaid expenses (e.g., prepaid rent) means the expense was recognized and deducted from PBT, but the cash payment occurred in a prior period. To convert PBT to a cash basis, this amount is **added back** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-3].

    *   **Increase in Current Liabilities:** **Add** to PBT. This signifies expenses recorded without using cash (e.g., Accounts Payable increased because an expense was recorded on credit) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-23, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-24, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-4].
    *   **Decrease in Current Liabilities:** **Deduct** from PBT. This implies cash was used to pay off existing payables, but no new expense was recorded in the current PBT [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-23, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-24, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-7, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-8].
        *   **Example: Decrease in Accounts Payable (AP)**: A reduction in AP indicates cash was used to pay existing payables, but no additional expense was recorded in PBT. This cash used must be **deducted** from PBT [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-4].
        *   **Example: Decrease in Accrued Expenses**: Similar to AP, a decrease in accrued expenses (e.g., wages payable) means cash was used to pay these expenses, but no corresponding expense was recorded in the current PBT. This amount is **deducted** from PBT [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-5].

3.  **Non-operating Items (Gains/Losses on disposal of assets):**
    *   **Gains on disposal of long-term assets:** **Deduct** from PBT. These are non-operating gains already included in PBT, but the cash transaction (sale of asset) is part of investing activities. Deducting the gain avoids double-counting [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-23].
    *   **Losses on disposal of long-term assets:** **Add back** to PBT. These are non-operating losses already deducted from PBT, but the cash transaction (sale of asset) is part of investing activities. Adding back the loss avoids double-counting [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-23, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-33].

4.  **Interest, Dividends, and Income Taxes (Separately Reported):**
    *   **Interest Income:** First, **deduct** the accrual-basis interest income from PBT to remove it. Then, calculate the actual cash received for interest and **add it back** to PBT. Using T-account reconstruction for Interest Receivable helps determine the cash received [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-6, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-34].
    *   **Interest Expense:** First, **add back** the accrual-basis interest expense to PBT. Then, calculate the actual cash paid for interest and **deduct** that amount from PBT. T-account reconstruction for Interest Payable helps determine the cash paid [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-6].
    *   **Income Taxes Paid:** Since PBT is the starting point (Profit *BEFORE* Tax), income tax recognized under accrual basis is not included. The only thing to do is figure out the actual taxes paid in cash during the period and **deduct** that amount from PBT [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-7, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-23, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-35]. Income taxes paid are a cash outflow and are subtracted from PBT [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-23].

**Summary Table for Adjusting Profit to CFO** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-24]:

| Change in Account Balance During Year | Increase          | Decrease        |
| :------------------------------------ | :---------------- | :-------------- |
| **Noncash Current Assets**            | Subtract from profit | Add to profit   |
| **Current Liabilities**               | Add to profit     | Subtract from profit |

*   If a current asset account has increased, the increase is subtracted from Net Income (NI) (e.g., ending balance $50k – beginning balance $30k = $20k to be subtracted from NI).
*   If a current asset account has decreased, the decrease is added to NI (e.g., ending balance $20k – beginning balance $30k = -$10k; subtracting a negative amount results in an addition to NI).
*   For liabilities, increases are added to and decreases are subtracted from NI [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-24].

### WildLuwak Coffee - Preparing CFO Section

To prepare the CFO section using the Indirect Method:
1.  **Step 1:** Use the comparative Statement of Financial Position (SFP) to calculate the account changes for each item in the SFP, and identify whether they relate to CFO, CFI, or CFF [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-27, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-29].
2.  **Step 2:** Take the Profit Before Tax (PBT) from the Income Statement (IS) to begin the SCF [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-27, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-30].
3.  **Step 3:** Prepare the CFO section by making the necessary adjustments to PBT [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-27, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-31].

**Example Adjustments using WildLuwak Coffee:**

*   **Depreciation Expense:** Add back depreciation expense to PBT. This is a non-cash operating item [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-31].
*   **Changes in Non-cash Current Assets & Liabilities:** Apply the rules from the summary table:
    *   Accounts Receivable: If AR increased by $4,549, it is deducted from PBT [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-32].
    *   Inventories: If Inventories increased by $858, it is deducted from PBT [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-32].
    *   Prepaid Expenses: If Prepaid Expenses decreased by $6,457, it is added to PBT [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-32].
    *   Accounts Payable: If AP decreased by $1,798, it is deducted from PBT [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-32].
    *   Accrued Expenses: If Accrued Expenses decreased by $396, it is deducted from PBT [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-32].
*   **Loss on Disposal of Asset:** A loss on disposal of asset (e.g., $2,500) is a non-operating item and an investing activity, not part of CFO. Thus, it is added back to PBT, as it will be accounted for later in CFI [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-33].
*   **Interest Income and Income Taxes Paid:**
    *   Interest income (e.g., $860) is first deducted from PBT (to remove its accrual basis impact), and then the actual cash received is added back. If there's no interest receivable outstanding, the entire reported interest income was received in cash [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-34].
    *   Income tax paid (e.g., $11,055) is directly deducted from PBT. The amount of taxes paid in cash is determined using the Income Tax Payable T-account, comparing beginning and ending balances with the income tax expense from the IS [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-35, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-post-lecture-supplement-pdf#section-7].

After all adjustments, the "Net cash from operating activities" is calculated [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-36].

## Analyzing & Interpreting CFO

CFO is considered the most important section of the SCF because operations are typically a company's major source of cash. It highlights a firm's ability to generate cash internally through operations and its management of current assets and liabilities (working capital) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-37].

A common rule of thumb for financial and credit analysts is to be wary of firms with rising net income but falling cash flow from operations.
*   **Investors** evaluate CFO to ensure sufficient cash for dividends or company expansion.
*   **Creditors** assess CFO to confirm a company's ability to repay loans [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-37].

**AR & Inventory Changes as Signals:**
*   **Accounts Receivable Changes:** Managers might increase credit terms or lower credit standards to boost declining sales. An increase in AR can lead to net income outpacing cash flows from operations, signaling potential issues with cash collection [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-38].
*   **Inventory Changes:** Inventory growth could indicate that planned sales growth did not materialize. Conversely, a decline in inventory might suggest that the company anticipates lower sales in the next quarter [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-38].

## Cash Flows from Investing Activities (CFI) - Preparing the CFI

Determining cash provided or used in investing activities follows a three-stage process:
1.  Identify changes in investing-related accounts.
2.  Explain these changes using reconstruction analysis (e.g., T-accounts).
3.  Report their cash flow effects [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-39].

### WildLuwak Coffee - Preparing CFI Section

To prepare the CFI section:
1.  **Step 1:** Use the comparative SFP to calculate account changes for CFI items. However, relying solely on SFP changes for PPE and Accumulated Depreciation is insufficient to understand the cash paid/received from purchases and disposals [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-40, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-41].
2.  **Step 2:** Complete the CFI section using additional information on investing activities, such as purchase/sale of fixed assets or investments [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-40].

**Example from WildLuwak Coffee:**
*   **Purchase of new PPE:** If the company purchased $10,000 new PPE in cash, this is a cash outflow for investing activities [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-40, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-42].

Journal entries and T-account reconstruction for PPE and Accumulated Depreciation are used to determine the cash received, depreciation expense, and any gain or loss on disposal [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-40, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-42].

## Cash Flows from Financing Activities (CFF) - Preparing the CFF

Determining cash provided or used in financing activities also follows a three-stage process:
1.  Identify changes in financing-related accounts.
2.  Explain these changes using reconstruction analysis.
3.  Report their cash flow effects [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-43].

### WildLuwak Coffee - Preparing CFF Section

To prepare the CFF section:

Similar to CFI, SFP changes alone for Share Capital and Retained Earnings are insufficient without additional information [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-44, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-45].
2.  **Step 2:** Complete the CFF section using additional information on financing activities, such as sale/repurchase of stock, dividend payments, and borrowings [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-44].

## Final Step: Completing the SCF

To complete the SCF:
1.  Calculate the net increase or decrease in cash for the year by summing the net cash from Operating Activities (CFO), Investing Activities (CFI), and Financing Activities (CFF) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-47].
2.  Add this net change to the beginning cash balance. The resulting ending cash balance **must** tie to the ending cash balance reported on the Statement of Financial Position [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-47].

## Analyzing & Interpreting CFF

The CFF provides insight into how management has chosen to fund the company's growth. Long-term growth is typically financed from three sources:
*   Internally generated funds (from operations).
*   Issuance of stock.
*   Money borrowed on a long-term basis [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-48].

By examining the CFF, analysts can understand a company's capital structure and the types of external financing used (from shareholders or creditors). This information is crucial for evaluating a business's growth potential [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-48].

## Using SCF for Decision Making: Identifying Patterns from SCF

Analyzing patterns in cash flows from operating, investing, and financing activities can reveal a company's financial health and strategic direction [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-49].

## Appendix: Algebraic Formulation of the SCF

The Statement of Cash Flows can be derived from the basic accounting equation:
Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-55].

Separating assets into Cash (C) and Non-cash Assets (NCA):
Cash + Non-cash assets = Liabilities + SE [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-55].

Considering changes in balances ($\Delta$):
$\Delta$ Cash + $\Delta$ Non-cash assets = $\Delta$ Liabilities + $\Delta$ SE [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-55].

Rearranging to isolate $\Delta$ Cash:
$\Delta$ Cash = ($\Delta$ Liabilities + $\Delta$ SE) - $\Delta$ Non-cash assets [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-55].

Further expanding this equation to differentiate between current and non-current items, and then breaking down $\Delta$ Liabilities and $\Delta$ SE into their components (like Net Income, dividends, new debt, asset purchases/sales, depreciation, gains/losses), allows for the algebraic derivation of cash flows from Operating, Investing, and Financing activities. This formulation shows how changes in non-cash accounts ultimately explain the change in the cash account [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-11-slides-statement-of-cash-flows-pdf#section-56].
