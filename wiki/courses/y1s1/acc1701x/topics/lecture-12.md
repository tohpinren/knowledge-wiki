# Lecture 12

This page consolidates the numbered lecture materials for ACC1701X. Accounting for Decision Makers Lecture 12 provides a comprehensive recap of core accounting concepts from previous lectures, including the accounting equation, debit/credit rules, accrual accounting, financial statement preparation, and closing temporary accounts. It then introduces Financial Statement Analysis (FSA), covering its purpose, key building blocks, and various analytical tools like vertical analysis, horizontal analysis, ratio analysis (profitability, liquidity & efficiency, solvency, market prospects, cash flow), and the DuPont Framework. The lecture concludes by discussing the limitations of financial statement and ratio analysis [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-1, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-4, acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-5].

## Lecture 1-11 Quick Refresher

This lecture begins with a comprehensive recap of key accounting concepts covered in the preceding eleven lectures [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-1].

1.  **Accounting Equation**
    *   Assets = Liabilities + Equity [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-1]
2.  **Debit/Credit Rules for T-accounts and Journal Entries**
    *   Assets increase with a debit, while Liabilities and Equity increase with a credit.
    *   Revenue increases with a credit, and expenses increase with a debit [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-1].
3.  **Accrual Accounting**
    *   Adjusting Journal Entries (AJEs) are used to record revenues and expenses in the correct accounting period [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-1].
4.  **Financial Statement Preparation & Closing the Books**
    *   The process involves preparing the Income Statement, Statement of Changes in Equity, and Statement of Financial Position.
    *   Temporary accounts (revenues, expenses, and dividends) are closed to Retained Earnings at period-end [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-1].
5.  **Financial Statements Integrity**
    *   This includes procedures for fixing errors in financial statements and implementing [[lecture-05#Internal Control System|internal controls]] [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-1].
6.  **Receivables**
    *   Covers the allowance for impairment loss and bad debt expense.
    *   Determining the target ending balance of the allowance account often uses an accounts receivable (AR) aging analysis.
    *   Movements in the allowance account (beginning balance, write-offs) help calculate the period's bad debt expense.
    *   Write-offs reduce the allowance account but do not impact Net Income [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-1].
7.  **Cash & Current Liabilities**
    *   [[lecture-07#Bank Reconciliation|Bank reconciliation]] involves following nine steps to reconcile bank and book balances.
    *   Includes understanding known liabilities, estimated liabilities (e.g., warranty liabilities), and contingent liabilities [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-1].
8.  **Merchandising Inventory**
    *   **Perpetual Inventory System**: Directly records purchases, discounts, and Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) into the Inventory account.
    *   **Periodic Inventory System**: COGS is calculated at the end of the period using the COGS equation.
    *   Common inventory costing methods include FIFO (First-In, First-Out), Units of Production, and Average Weighted Cost [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-1].
9.  **Property, Plant & Equipment (PPE) and Intangible Assets**
    *   **Tangible Assets (PPE)**: Depreciated using methods such as straight-line, units of production, and declining balance. Changes in depreciation estimates require recalculation moving forward.
    *   **Intangible Assets**: Amortized using the straight-line method for intangibles with a definite life [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-1].
10. **Equity**
    *   Includes the issuance of [[lecture-10#Types of Shares|ordinary shares and preferred shares]].
    *   Distinction between par value and no-par value shares.
    *   Repurchase of shares results in [[lecture-10#Treasury Stock|treasury shares]].
    *   Covers both cash and stock dividends [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-1].
11. **Statement of Cash Flows**
    *   Classifies cash flows into three main activities:
        *   [[lecture-11#Cash Flows from Operating Activities (CFO)|Cash Flows from Operations (CFO)]] (Indirect Method).
        *   [[lecture-11#Cash Flows from Investing Activities (CFI)|Cash Flows from Investing (CFI)]].
        *   [[lecture-11#Cash Flows from Financing Activities (CFF)|Cash Flows from Financing (CFF)]] [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-1].

## Financial Statement Analysis (FSA)

Financial Statement Analysis (FSA) is the process of examining a company's financial statements to make informed economic decisions [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2].

### Why Analyze Financial Statements? (LO1)

Companies spend billions preparing, auditing, and publishing financial statements because they help users make better economic decisions [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2].

*   **External Users**: Include investors (current stockholders and potential investors), financial analysts, creditors, and customers.
*   **Internal Users**: Primarily management [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2].

FSA involves examining the relationships between financial numbers, their trends, and various amounts across financial statements. It serves two main purposes [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2]:

1.  **Diagnostic Purposes**: Helps users evaluate and identify problems within a company.
2.  **Prognostic Purposes**: Enables users to predict future performance based on a company's past performance [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2].

### Building Blocks of Financial Analysis

The four main elements of a company's financial performance that are analyzed are [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2]:

1.  **Profitability**: Measures a company's ability to generate an adequate return on invested capital [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2, acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-1].
2.  **Liquidity / Efficiency**:
    *   **Liquidity**: A company's ability to meet its short-term (current) obligations [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2, acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-2].
    *   **Efficiency**: Measures how productive and efficient a company is in using its assets [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2, acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-2].
3.  **Solvency**: A company's ability to meet its long-term obligations [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2, acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-3].
4.  **Market Prospects**: Ratios that measure the market worth of a share of stock, useful for analyzing public companies [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2, acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-4].

### Standards for Comparison

Financial statements cannot be evaluated in isolation; they must be compared to appropriate benchmarks [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2]:

1.  **Intracompany (Time Series Analysis)**: Examines a single company's financial data over time to identify trends (e.g., sales growth year-to-year) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2].
2.  **Comparison with Similar Companies (Competitors)**: Provides insights into a company's relative performance by comparing it to competitors exposed to similar industry factors [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2].
3.  **Industry Benchmarks**: Industry statistics from sources like Dun & Bradstreet, Standard & Poor's, or Moody's serve as comparison standards [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2].

### FSA Analysis Tools

There are three common types of financial statement analysis [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2]:

1.  **Vertical Analysis**: Compares a company's performance to a base amount [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2].
2.  **Horizontal Analysis**: Compares a company's performance across time [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2].
3.  **Ratio Analysis**: Measures the proportional relationship between two or more financial statement numbers. This includes **DuPont Analysis**, a framework to decompose the Return on Equity (ROE) ratio [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2].

#### 1. Vertical Analysis (LO2) - Common-size Statements

Common-size statements express each item on a financial statement as a percentage of a single base amount, also known as component percentages [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2].

*   **Formula**:
    $$ \text{Common-size Percent} = \frac{\text{Analysis amount}}{\text{Base amount}} \times 100 $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2]
*   For the **Balance Sheet**, the base amount is **Total Assets**. Each balance sheet item is divided by total assets.
*   For the **Income Statement**, the base amount is **Net Sales Revenue** [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2].

#### 2. Horizontal Analysis (LO2) - Comparative Statements

Horizontal analysis compares financial data across two or more successive periods to capture trends [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2].

*   **Dollar Change**: Change from year to year (typically prior year is the base year) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2].
    $$ \text{Dollar Change} = \text{Analysis period amount} - \text{Base period amount} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2]
*   **Percent Change**: Change from year to year in percentages [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2].
    $$ \text{Percent Change} = \frac{\text{Analysis period amount} - \text{Base period amount}}{\text{Base period amount}} \times 100 $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2]
*   **Trend Analysis**: Helps reveal patterns over successive periods [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2].
    $$ \text{Trend Percent} = \frac{\text{Analysis period amount}}{\text{Base period amount}} \times 100 $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-2]

#### 3. Ratio Analysis (LO3)

Ratio analysis is a widely used tool that identifies trends and conditions within a company, though its usefulness depends on the user's interpretation [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]. Ratios are based on the four building blocks of financial analysis: profitability, liquidity & efficiency, solvency, and market prospects [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].

##### A. Profitability Ratios

These ratios measure a company's ability to generate an adequate return on invested capital by comparing income to other financial statement items [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3, acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-1].

*   **Return on Equity (ROE)**: Measures income earned per dollar of shareholder investment [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3, acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-1].
    $$ \text{Return on Equity (ROE)} = \frac{\text{Net Income} - \text{Preferred Dividends}}{\text{Average Ordinary Shareholders' Equity}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]
*   **Return on Assets (ROA)**: Measures income earned per dollar of assets, indicating management's effectiveness in utilizing assets to generate income [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3, acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-1].
    $$ \text{Return on Assets (ROA)} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Average Total Assets}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]
*   **Net Profit Margin**: Measures the percentage of profit generated from each dollar of sales, indicating overall company efficiency [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{Net Profit Margin} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Net Sales}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]
*   **Gross Profit Margin**: Measures a company's efficiency in selling above its production cost [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{Gross Profit Margin} = \frac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Net Sales}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]
*   **Operating Profit Margin**: Measures a company's operating efficiency from its main operations [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{Operating Profit Margin} = \frac{\text{Operating Income}}{\text{Net Sales}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]
*   **Earnings per Share (EPS)**: Measures the profitability of a company by its ability to produce income for each ordinary share outstanding. It is a required disclosure on the Income Statement. Companies must also disclose diluted EPS, which assumes conversion of all convertible securities into ordinary shares [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{Basic EPS} = \frac{\text{Net Income} - \text{Preferred Dividends}}{\text{Weighted-average Ordinary Shares Outstanding}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]

##### B. Liquidity & Efficiency Ratios

*   **Liquidity** measures a company's ability to meet its short-term (current) obligations, focusing on the relationship between current assets and current liabilities [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3, acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-2].
*   **Efficiency** measures how productive or efficient a company is in using its assets [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3, acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-2].

These ratios include [source:acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-2]:

*   **Current Ratio**: Measures a firm's ability to pay current obligations with current assets [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{Current Ratio} = \frac{\text{Current Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]
*   **Acid-Test Ratio (Quick Ratio)**: A more stringent test of short-term liquidity, considering only "quick assets" (cash + short-term financial assets + current receivables) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{Acid-test Ratio} = \frac{\text{Quick Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]
*   **Accounts Receivable Turnover Ratio**: Measures how effective the company is at collecting cash from its customers [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{Accounts Receivables Turnover} = \frac{\text{Net Sales}}{\text{Average Net Receivables}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]
*   **Days' Sales Uncollected**: Measures the average days it takes to collect cash from credit customers [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{Days' Sales Uncollected} = \frac{365}{\text{Accounts Receivable Turnover}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]
*   **Inventory Turnover Ratio**: Measures the number of times merchandise is sold and replaced during the year, used to assess inventory control [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{Inventory Turnover} = \frac{\text{COGS}}{\text{Average Inventory}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]
*   **Days' Sales in Inventory**: Measures the average days it takes to sell inventory, useful for assessing inventory liquidity [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{Days' Sales in Inventory} = \frac{365}{\text{Inventory Turnover}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]
*   **Days' Purchases in Accounts Payable**: Measures the average days it takes a company to pay cash to its suppliers [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{Days' Purchases in AP} = \frac{365}{\text{Purchases / Average AP}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]
*   **Total Asset Turnover**: Measures a company's ability to use its assets to generate sales [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{Total Asset Turnover} = \frac{\text{Net Sales}}{\text{Average Total Assets}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]
*   **Fixed Asset (PPE) Turnover**: Measures a company's ability to use its fixed assets (PPE) to generate sales [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{Fixed Asset Turnover} = \frac{\text{Net Sales}}{\text{Average Fixed Assets (PPE)}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]

**Working Capital**: Represents current assets that do not require near-term repayment. More working capital suggests a strong liquidity position and ability to meet current obligations [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].
$$ \text{Working Capital} = \text{Current assets} - \text{Current liabilities} $$
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]

##### C. Solvency Ratios

Solvency is a company's ability to meet its long-term obligations. Analysis of a company's capital structure (how reliant it is on debt) is a crucial component of solvency analysis, as heavy reliance on debt increases risk [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3, acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-3].

*   **Times Interest Earned Ratio**: Indicates how many times a company can pay its interest obligations from its income before interest and tax [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{Times Interest Earned} = \frac{\text{Earnings before Interest and Tax (EBIT)}}{\text{Interest Expense}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]
*   **Debt Ratio**: Measures how much liabilities a company has relative to its assets, assessing its financial leverage [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3, acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{Debt Ratio} = \frac{\text{Total Liabilities}}{\text{Total Assets}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]
*   **Debt-to-Equity Ratio**: Measures how much liabilities a company has relative to its equity. A value of 1.0 means borrowing equals shareholder investment [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3, acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{Debt-to-Equity Ratio} = \frac{\text{Total Liabilities}}{\text{Total Equity}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]

##### D. Market Prospects Ratios

These ratios measure the market worth of a share of stock and are useful for analyzing public companies. They reflect the market's expectations of a company's return and risk based on the current value of the owner's investment [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3, acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-4].

*   **Price-to-Earnings Ratio (P/E Ratio)**: Measures a company's current share price relative to its per-share earnings (also known as "price multiple"). A high P/E ratio suggests the market expects high growth or could indicate overvaluation [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{P/E Ratio} = \frac{\text{Market Price per Ordinary Share}}{\text{Earnings per Share (EPS)}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]
*   **Dividend Yield Ratio**: Measures the amount of cash dividends distributed to ordinary shares relative to market value. It helps assess an investor's return on investment based on dividends [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{Dividend Yield} = \frac{\text{Dividends per Share}}{\text{Market Price per Share}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]

##### E. Cash Flow Ratios

Cash flow ratios are important because:
*   Large non-cash expenses (e.g., depreciation, write-offs) can make Net Income appear lower, not reflecting a positive cash performance.
*   Rapidly growing companies often burn cash operationally and require sufficient cash flow.
*   Cash flow is less susceptible to manipulation compared to Net Income, which can be affected by accrual accounting assumptions and "window dressing" [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3, acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-5].

*   **Cash Flow to Net Income**: Reflects the extent to which accrual accounting assumptions and adjustments are included in computing net income [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3].
    $$ \text{Cash Flow to Net Income} = \frac{\text{Cash Flow from Operations}}{\text{Net Income}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]
*   **Cash Flow Adequacy**: Assesses if a company generates enough cash flow from operations to cover capital expenditures (CAPEX) in PPE and still have cash remaining for liabilities or stockholders. A business with excess operating cash is often called a "cash cow" [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]. For this module, CAPEX only includes PPE acquisitions [source:acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-5].
    $$ \text{Cash Flow Adequacy} = \frac{\text{Cash Flow from Operations}}{\text{Cash paid for CAPEX}} $$
    [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-3]

#### DuPont Framework (LO4)

The DuPont Framework is a systematic approach to break down Return on Equity (ROE) into three key components: profitability, efficiency, and leverage [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-4, acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-5].

$$ \text{Return on Equity (ROE)} = \text{Profitability} \times \text{Efficiency} \times \text{Leverage} $$
$$ \text{ROE} = \left(\frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Net Sales}}\right) \times \left(\frac{\text{Net Sales}}{\text{Average Total Assets}}\right) \times \left(\frac{\text{Average Total Assets}}{\text{Average Total Equity}}\right) $$
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-4]

Where:
*   **Profitability** (Net Income / Net Sales): Represents the company's ability to generate net income per dollar of sales [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-4].
*   **Efficiency** (Net Sales / Average Total Assets): Represents the company's ability to generate sales through the use of assets (also known as Asset Turnover) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-4].
*   **Leverage** (Average Total Assets / Average Total Equity): Represents the degree to which a company uses borrowed funds instead of invested funds (also known as Assets-to-equity ratio) [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-4].

When extracting figures from consolidated financial statements, amounts "attributable to the owners" (i.e., belonging to the shareholders of the parent company) should be used [source:acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-5]. Thus, for ROE attributable to ordinary shareholders, the DuPont analysis is [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-4, acc1701x-lecture-post-lecture-12-supplement-pdf#section-5]:

$$ \text{Return on Equity} = \left(\frac{\text{Net Income (attributable to ordinary shareholders)}}{\text{Net Sales}}\right) \times \left(\frac{\text{Net Sales}}{\text{Average Total Assets}}\right) \times \left(\frac{\text{Average Total Assets}}{\text{Average Equity (attributable to ordinary shareholders)}}\right) $$
[source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-4]

### Limitations of Financial Statement and Ratio Analysis (LO5)

Several cautionary notes should be considered when conducting financial statement and ratio analysis [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-5]:

*   **Lack of Standardisation**: Metrics and ratios are generally *not* governed by financial reporting standards, with the exception of EPS and diluted EPS. There is no single standard that all analysts follow, and analysts often develop their own unique metrics [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-5].
*   **Susceptibility to Manipulation**: Ratios can be susceptible to "manipulation" or "window dressing" where companies might alter their financial statements to generate seemingly "good" or acceptable ratios, or to highlight unusual metrics, potentially misleading investors [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-5].
*   **Skepticism**: Always be skeptical when analyzing financial statements. Watch out for unexplained large changes in financial statement items that are described in vague terms such as "other operating expenses" or "other payables" [source:acc1701x-lecture-lecture-12-slides-analyzing-fs-pdf#section-5].
