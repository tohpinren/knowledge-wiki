# CS1231S

CS1231S is a foundational course in discrete structures, aiming to develop mathematical maturity and provide essential mathematical prerequisites for advanced computer science modules [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-lecture00-welcome-20s1-pdf#section-2]. The module covers a broad range of topics, including logic, proof methods, set theory, functions, relations, number theory, induction, recursion, counting, probability, and graph theory [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-lecture00-welcome-20s1-pdf#section-2, cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-5-sets-1-pdf#section-1, cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-rev-1-pdf#section-1]. The primary textbook for the course is Susanna S. Epp's *Discrete Mathematics With Applications* [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-susanna-s-epp-discrete-mathematics-with-applications-2019-cengage-pdf#page-1, cs1231s-susanna-s-epp-discrete-mathematics-with-applications-metric-version-pdf#page-1].

*   **Midterm Test:** An open-book assessment, typically 1 hour 30 minutes long, with a maximum of 50 marks [source:cs1231s-midterm-submission-midterm-release-for-students-pdf#section-1].
*   **Final Examination:** A 2-hour, open-book examination worth 100 marks, requiring answers in a provided booklet [source:cs1231s-finals-cs1231s-exam20s1-qns-pdf#section-1, cs1231s-finals-cs1231s-exam19s1-qns-pdf#section-1].
*   **Tutorial Attendance:** Mandatory and contributes 5% to the final grade. Documented absences are required for valid reasons [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-tutorials-tutorial-1-tut01qns-pdf#section-1].

All submissions typically require a single PDF file named with the student number [source:cs1231s-assignment-assign1-qns-pdf#section-1, cs1231s-assignment-assign2-qns-pdf#section-1].

## Topics

### Mathematical Logic
The module begins with an introduction to logical reasoning, covering mathematical logic. Key components include:
*   **[[lecture-01-mathematical-language|Lecture 01: Mathematical Language and Statements]]**: Defines the language of mathematics, including variables, statements, and sentences, to express mathematical properties clearly [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-lecture01-speaking-mathematically-full-pdf#section-1].
*   **[[lecture-02-logical-equivalence|Lecture 02: Logical Equivalence and Laws]]**: Introduces propositional logic, defining statements as sentences that are definitively true or false, and covers logical connectives (`~`, `∧`, `∨`, `→`, `↔`), logical equivalence, tautologies, and contradictions [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-lecture02-logicofcompoundstatements-full-pdf#section-1, cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-lecture02-logicofcompoundstatements-summary-pdf#section-1].
*   **[[lecture-03-predicates-quantifiers|Lecture 03: Predicates, Quantifiers and Truth Sets]]**: Extends logic to predicates (sentences with variables) and quantifiers (universal `∀` and existential `∃`), which form quantified statements, along with the concept of a truth set [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-lecture03-logicofquantifiedstatements-full-pdf#section-1, cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-lecture03-logicofquantifiedstatements-summary-pdf#section-1].

### Methods of Proof
A core aspect of CS1231S is developing rigorous proof methods. Students learn various techniques for proving and disproving mathematical statements:
*   **[[lecture-04-direct-proof-disproof|Lecture 04: Direct Proof and Disproof]]**: Covers fundamental definitions for integers (even, odd, prime, composite, rational) and methods of direct proof and disproof, including using counterexamples [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-lecture04-methods-of-proof-full-pdf#section-1]. Other methods include proof by contraposition and proof by contradiction [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-finals-cs1231-s-cheatsheet-nil-pdf#section-1].

### Set Theory and Functions
Set Theory forms a fundamental building block for discrete mathematics:
*   **[[lecture-05-set-operations|Lecture 05: Set Operations and Laws]]**: Defines sets as unordered collections of objects (elements), introduces common number sets ($\mathbb{N}$, $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$), and covers basic set operations such as subsets, power sets, union, intersection, and Cartesian products, along with their associated laws [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-notes5-sets-pdf#section-1, cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-indices-pdf#section-1, cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-5-sets-0-pdf#section-1].
*   **[[lecture-06-types-functions-composition|Lecture 06: Types of Functions and Composition]]**: Defines functions (or maps) by their domain, codomain, and range. It explores types of functions, including injective (one-to-one), surjective (onto), and bijective functions, as well as function composition and inverse functions [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-6-1-fns-0-pdf#section-1, cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-6-2-bij-1-pdf#section-1].

### Induction and Recursion
Induction and recursion are powerful tools in discrete mathematics:
*   **[[lecture-07-strong-induction-recursion|Lecture 07: Strong Induction and Recursive Definitions]]**: Covers the principles of mathematical induction (MI) and strong mathematical induction (Strong MI) as deductive proof methods. It also introduces recursively defined sequences (e.g., Factorial, Fibonacci) and sets, and the application of structural induction [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-7-1-indn-0-pdf#section-1, cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-notes7-indn-pdf#section-1, cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-7-2-recn-0-pdf#section-1].

### Number Theory
Number theory covers properties of integers:
*   **[[lecture-08-fta|Lecture 08: Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic]]**: Introduces divisibility (`d | n`), floor and ceiling functions, prime and composite numbers, the Division Algorithm, and the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, which states that every integer $n \ge 2$ has a unique prime factorization [source:2pages-pdf#section-1, cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-8-1-divprime-1-pdf#section-1]. It also covers base-$b$ representation, Greatest Common Divisors (GCD) using the Euclidean Algorithm, Bézout's Lemma, and modular arithmetic concepts like congruence and multiplicative inverses [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-8-2-baseb-0-pdf#section-1, cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-8-3-gcdfta-0-pdf#section-1, cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-8-4-mod-0-pdf#section-1].

### Relations
The module explores the concept of relations between elements of sets:
*   **[[lecture-09-properties-relations|Lecture 09: Properties of Relations]]**: Defines relations as subsets of Cartesian products and discusses their properties: reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, and antisymmetry [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-notes9-relns-pdf#section-1]. It further differentiates between equivalence relations (reflexive, symmetric, transitive) and partial orders (reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive), also covering partitions, equivalence classes, total orders, and maximal/minimal elements [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-9-1-equiv-1-pdf#section-1, cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-9-2-po-0-pdf#section-1].

### Counting and Probability
Counting and probability are essential for analyzing discrete systems:
*   **[[lecture-10-counting-principles|Lecture 10: Sum, Product, and Pigeonhole Principles]]**: Covers fundamental counting principles, including the sum rule, product rule, difference rule, inclusion-exclusion principle, and the Pigeonhole Principle, which are applied to solve various combinatorial problems [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-lecture10-counting1-pdf#section-1, cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-tutorials-tutorial-9-tut09-enzio-pdf#section-1].
*   **[[lecture-11-expected-conditional-probability|Lecture 11: Expected Value and Conditional Probability]]**: Introduces advanced counting techniques such as permutations and combinations, including those with repetition, and Pascal's Formula. It also covers fundamental probability concepts like sample spaces, events, equally likely probability, conditional probability, Bayes' Theorem, and expected value [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-lecture11-counting2-pdf#section-1, cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-lecture11-counting2-summary-pdf#section-1].

### Graph Theory
Graph theory provides tools for modeling and analyzing relationships:
*   **[[lecture-12-types-connectivity-graphs|Lecture 12: Types of Graphs and Connectivity]]**: Defines graphs as mathematical structures comprising vertices and edges, used to model pairwise relations. It distinguishes between undirected, directed, simple, complete, and bipartite graphs, and covers concepts like connectivity, paths, circuits, and graph complements [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-lecture12-graphs-pdf#section-1, cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-lecture12-graphs-summary-pdf#section-1].
*   **[[lecture-13-rooted-trees-traversals|Lecture 13: Rooted Trees and Traversals]]**: Introduces trees as connected, circuit-free graphs and defines related concepts such as forests, terminal (leaf) vertices, internal vertices, and various types of rooted and binary trees. It covers properties like level, height, parent, child, sibling, and ancestor, along with tree traversal algorithms (pre-order, in-order, post-order) [source:cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-lecture13-trees-pdf#section-1, cs1231s-cs1231s-extra-lecture-slides-lecture13-trees-summary-pdf#section-1].
