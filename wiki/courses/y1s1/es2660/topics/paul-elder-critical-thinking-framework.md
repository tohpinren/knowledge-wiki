# Paul & Elder's Critical Thinking Model

Paul and Elder's Critical Thinking Model presents a comprehensive framework for understanding and developing critical thinking as a self-directed, self-disciplined, self-monitored, and self-corrective process. This model emphasizes the cultivation of intellectual abilities and virtues, recognizing innate barriers to rationality, and fostering critical societies [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-1, es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-2, es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-3].

---

## Introduction to Critical Thinking

While thinking is a natural human activity, much of it can be biased, distorted, or uninformed. The quality of our lives and what we produce directly depends on the quality of our thought. Poor thinking incurs significant costs, making the systematic cultivation of excellence in thought essential [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-3].

### Definition
Critical thinking is defined as the art of analyzing and evaluating thought processes to improve them. It is self-directed, self-disciplined, self-monitored, and self-corrective thinking, requiring rigorous standards and a mindful command of their use. It involves effective communication, problem-solving abilities, and a commitment to overcoming innate biases like egocentrism and sociocentrism, ultimately advancing ethical sensitivities and intellectual virtues [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-3].

### Characteristics of a Well-Cultivated Critical Thinker
A well-cultivated critical thinker [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-3]:
*   Formulates vital questions and problems with clarity and precision.
*   Gathers and assesses relevant information, using abstract ideas effectively for interpretation.
*   Arrives at well-reasoned conclusions and solutions, testing them against relevant criteria.
*   Thinks open-mindedly within alternative systems of thought, evaluating their assumptions, implications, and consequences.
*   Communicates effectively when solving complex problems.
*   Is scrupulously careful not to misrepresent or distort information, and identifies false information.

## Stages of Critical Thinking Development

Paul and Elder identify six stages in the development of critical thinking [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-3]:
1.  **Unreflective Thinkers:** Unaware of significant problems in their thinking.
2.  **Challenged Thinkers:** Faced with significant problems in their thinking.
3.  **Beginning Thinkers:** Attempt to improve but without regular practice.
4.  **Practicing Thinkers:** Regularly practice and advance accordingly.
5.  **Advanced Thinkers:** Committed to lifelong practice and beginning to internalize intellectual virtues.
6.  **Accomplished Thinkers:** Intellectual skills and virtues have become second nature.

## A Substantive Approach to Critical Thinking

A robust understanding of critical thinking involves the disciplined analysis and assessment of reasoning, alongside the cultivation of intellectual virtues. This approach highlights two primary barriers to criticality: egocentric and sociocentric thinking, which are widespread in human thought [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-3].

The substantive approach integrates three core components:
1.  **The Analysis of Thought:** Focused on the [elements of reasoning](#the-elements-of-thought-reasoning).
2.  **The Assessment of Thought:** Using [universal intellectual standards](#universal-intellectual-standards).
3.  **The Cultivation of Intellectual Traits:** Developing [intellectual virtues](#intellectual-virtues-of-the-fairminded-critical-thinker).

All of these are considered **with concern for the barriers to criticality**, namely egocentricity and sociocentricity [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-3].

## The Elements of Thought (Reasoning)

All reasoning contains eight fundamental structures. Whenever we think, we do so for a **purpose** within a **point of view**, based on **assumptions** that lead to **implications** and **consequences**. We use **concepts** to interpret **information** to answer **questions**, solve **problems**, and make **inferences** [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-4]. Critical thinkers apply universal intellectual standards to these elements [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-4].

### Questions Using the Elements of Thought
To analyze reasoning, consider the following questions for each element [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-4]:
*   **Purpose:** What am I trying to accomplish? What is my central aim or purpose?
*   **Questions:** What question am I raising or addressing? Am I considering the complexities?
*   **Information:** What information am I using? What information do I need? How do I know it's true?
*   **Inferences/Conclusions:** How did I reach this conclusion? Is there another way to interpret the information?
*   **Concepts:** What is the main idea here? Can I explain this idea?
*   **Assumptions:** What am I taking for granted? What assumption led to this conclusion?
*   **Implications/Consequences:** If my position is accepted, what are the important implications? What am I implying?
*   **Points of View:** From what point of view am I looking at this issue? Should I consider other viewpoints?

A more detailed checklist for reasoning expands on these questions, focusing on the clarity, fairness, and realism of purposes; the completeness and nature of questions; the justification of assumptions; the insights and weaknesses of viewpoints; the relevance and accuracy of information; the clarity and justification of concepts; the consistency and support for inferences; and the implications and consequences of reasoning [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-4].

### The Figuring Mind
There is a logic to figuring something out, to constructing meaning. Critical thinkers use intellectual standards to assess whether the logic in their minds accurately reflects the logic of the object being understood. The elements of thought (Purpose, Question at Issue, Assumptions, Conceptual Dimension, Inferences, Implications and Consequences, Point of View, Empirical Dimension) reveal this logic [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-4].

## Universal Intellectual Standards

To be effective thinkers, we must apply standards to the elements of reasoning to assess thought quality. Consistent application of these standards cultivates intellectual virtues. They must be explicitly taught and internalized to guide better reasoning [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5].

### Essential Intellectual Standards
*   **Clarity:** The gateway standard. If a statement is unclear, it cannot be assessed for accuracy or relevance. Questions: "Could you elaborate further?", "Could you give me an example?" [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5].
*   **Accuracy:** A statement can be clear but not accurate. Questions: "How could we check on that?", "How could we find out if that is true?" [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5].
*   **Precision:** A statement can be clear and accurate but lack specificity. Questions: "Could you be more specific?", "Could you give me more details?" [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5].
*   **Relevance:** A statement may be clear, accurate, and precise but not relevant to the issue. Questions: "How does that relate to the problem?", "How does that bear on the question?" [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5].
*   **Depth:** A statement can be clear, accurate, precise, and relevant, but superficial. It fails to deal with the complexities of the issue. Questions: "What factors make this a difficult problem?", "What are some of the complexities of this question?" [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5].
*   **Breadth:** Reasoning can be clear, accurate, precise, relevant, and deep but lack breadth by only considering one viewpoint. Questions: "Do we need to look at this from another perspective?", "Do we need to consider another point of view?" [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5].
*   **Logic:** When thoughts are mutually supporting and make sense, thinking is logical. If inconsistent or contradictory, it's illogical. Questions: "Does all this make sense together?", "Does what you say follow from the evidence?" [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5].
*   **Significance:** Focuses on the importance of the problem or central idea. Questions: "Is this the most important problem to consider?", "Which of these facts are most important?" [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5].
*   **Fairness:** Treating all relevant viewpoints without bias, acknowledging one's own feelings or interests. Questions: "Do I have any vested interest in this issue?", "Am I sympathetically representing the viewpoints of others?" [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5].
*   **Sufficiency:** Ensuring enough information is gathered to answer the question or solve the problem. Questions: "Do we have sufficient information to answer the question?", "Are we unfairly leaving out information we would rather not consider...?" [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5].

### Where Intellectual Standards Come From
Intellectual standards originate from the fundamental nature of thought itself and what we need thought to accomplish. For example, clarity is needed for communication, accuracy for understanding things as they are, and fairness to avoid biased reasoning [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5].

## Intellectual Virtues of the Fairminded Critical Thinker

Fairminded critical thinkers cultivate both intellectual abilities and dispositions, known as intellectual virtues, which are essential for excellent thought. These virtues help in pursuing one's goals while genuinely considering the rights and needs of others [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5].

### Essential Intellectual Traits (and their opposites)
*   **Intellectual Humility vs. Intellectual Arrogance:** Consciousness of the limits of one's knowledge and sensitivity to bias. It means not claiming to know more than one does. [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5]
*   **Intellectual Courage vs. Intellectual Cowardice:** Facing ideas, beliefs, or viewpoints that provoke strong negative emotions, and fairly addressing them. This includes recognizing potential truth in opposing views and falsity in strongly held group beliefs. [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5]
*   **Intellectual Empathy vs. Intellectual Narrow-mindedness:** Imaginatively putting oneself in others' shoes to genuinely understand their viewpoints, overcoming egocentric tendencies. [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5]
*   **Intellectual Autonomy vs. Intellectual Conformity:** Having rational control over one's beliefs, values, and inferences. It's about thinking for oneself, evaluating beliefs based on reason and evidence. [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5]
*   **Intellectual Integrity vs. Intellectual Hypocrisy:** Being true to one's own thinking, applying consistent intellectual standards, holding oneself to the same standards as others, and admitting inconsistencies. [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5]
*   **Intellectual Perseverance vs. Intellectual Laziness:** Using intellectual insights despite difficulties and frustrations, adhering to rational principles despite opposition, and struggling with complex questions for deeper understanding. [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5]
*   **Confidence in Reason vs. Distrust of Reason and Evidence:** Believing that one's own higher interests and those of humankind are best served by giving free rein to reason and by encouraging others to develop their rational faculties. [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5]
*   **Fairmindedness vs. Intellectual Unfairness:** Treating all viewpoints alike, without reference to one's own feelings or vested interests, or those of one's group. [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5]

### How Intellectual Virtues Are Interrelated
These intellectual virtues are interdependent. For example, intellectual humility requires intellectual courage to face one's prejudices, and intellectual empathy helps in fairmindedly reasoning with opposing viewpoints. Developing these virtues consistently and persistently is crucial for ethical critical reasoning [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-5].

## The Spirit of Critical Thinking

Critical thinkers have confidence in their ability to understand the logic of anything they encounter. They actively seek interrelationships and order within systems of ideas, using critical thinking tools daily to improve their thought processes and, consequently, their lives [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6].

## Tools for Critical Thinking

The Paul and Elder framework provides practical templates for applying critical thinking skills in various contexts:

### Template for Analyzing the Logic of an Article
This template helps deconstruct any article, essay, or textbook chapter by identifying [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6]:
1.  The main **purpose** of the article.
2.  The key **question** the author is addressing.
3.  The most important **information**.
4.  The main **inferences/conclusions**.
5.  The key **concepts**.
6.  The main **assumption(s)**.
7.  The **implications** if the reasoning is taken seriously or ignored.
8.  The main **point(s) of view**.

### Criteria for Evaluating Reasoning
Reasoning can be evaluated by asking specific questions related to each element of thought [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6]:
1.  **Purpose:** Is it clear, stated, and justifiable?
2.  **Question:** Is it well-stated, clear, unbiased, and relevant to the purpose?
3.  **Information:** Is it relevant, accurate, comprehensive, and not distorted?
4.  **Concepts:** Are key concepts clarified and used justifiably?
5.  **Assumptions:** Does the reasoner show sensitivity to assumptions and avoid questionable ones?
6.  **Inferences:** Is a logical line of reasoning developed, detailing how conclusions were reached?
7.  **Point of View:** Is there sensitivity to alternative viewpoints, and are objections addressed fairly?
8.  **Implications:** Is there sensitivity to the implications and consequences of the position?

### Template for Problem-Solving
To be an effective problem solver, one should [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6]:
1.  Regularly re-articulate goals, purposes, and needs, recognizing problems as obstacles.
2.  State each problem clearly and precisely.
3.  Study the problem to determine its "kind" (e.g., what needs to be done to solve it).
4.  Distinguish controllable problems from uncontrollable ones, focusing efforts appropriately.
5.  Figure out and actively seek necessary information.
6.  Carefully analyze and interpret information, drawing reasonable inferences.
7.  Determine short-term and long-term options for action, recognizing limitations.
8.  Evaluate options, identifying advantages and disadvantages.
9.  Adopt and follow through on a strategy (direct action or wait-and-see).
10. Monitor implications of actions and be ready to revise strategies or problem statements as new information emerges.

### Analyzing and Assessing Research
Effective research focuses on reasoning through issues, adhering to guidelines based on elements of reasoning and intellectual standards [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6]:
*   **Purpose:** Clear, explicitly distinguished, relevant, realistic, and significant.
*   **Question:** Clearly and precisely stated, articulated, relevant, realistic, significant, and defining clear intellectual tasks.
*   **Information:** Clear, accurate, relevant, sufficient, and explaining contrary information.
*   **Inferences:** Clear, accurate, relevant, consistent, reconciling discrepancies, and explaining how questions are settled.
*   **Point of View:** Identified, with objections from competing views fairly addressed.
*   **Assumptions:** Clearly identified, assessed, and explained how they shape the point of view.
*   **Concepts:** Assessed for clarity and significance.
*   **Implications:** Traced, searching for both negative and positive, and considering all significant ones.

## Three Kinds of Questions

Understanding the type of question is crucial for effective reasoning. Questions can be categorized into three kinds [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6]:
1.  **One-System Questions (Questions of Procedure):** These have established procedures or methods for finding a definitive answer, often settled by facts or definitions. They require evidence and reasoning within a single system, leading to a correct answer based on knowledge. Examples: "What is the boiling point of lead?"
2.  **No-System Questions (Questions of Preference):** These questions have as many answers as there are human preferences; subjective taste rules. They call for stating a subjective preference, cannot be assessed, and relate to personal opinion. Examples: "Which would you prefer: a vacation in the mountains or one at the seashore?"
3.  **Multi-System Questions (Questions of Judgment):** These require reasoning and have more than one arguable answer. They have "better-or-worse" answers, evaluated using intellectual standards like clarity and accuracy. They require evidence and reasoning within multiple, often conflicting, systems, leading to judgment. Examples: "How can we best address the most basic and significant economic problems of the nation today?"

## Three Levels of Thought

Reasoning can occur at different levels of quality [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6]:
1.  **Level 1: Lower Order Thinking:** Unreflective, low to mixed skill level, frequently relies on gut intuition, and is largely self-serving or self-deceived.
2.  **Level 2: Higher Order Thinking:** Selectively reflective, high skill level, but may lack critical thinking vocabulary and be inconsistently fair, potentially skilled in sophistry.
3.  **Level 3: Highest Order Thinking:** Explicitly reflective, highest skill level, routine use of critical thinking tools, and consistently fair. Achieving this requires cultivating both intellectual skills and virtues.

## The Human Mind: Irrationality and Rational Capacity

All humans possess innate egocentric and sociocentric tendencies, alongside underdeveloped rational capacities. Infantile egocentric (self-centered) thinking often merges with sociocentric (group-centered) thinking. Both forms of irrational thought are regularly engaged in, to varying degrees depending on the situation. Developing rational capacities is key to combating these irrational tendencies and fostering critical societies [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6].

### Humans Often Distort Reality Through Irrational Lenses
The mind employs "pathologies of thought" to deceive itself when pursuing irrational interests. These act as filters that [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6]:
*   Cause us to view the world based on perceived self-interests, disregarding others.
*   Distort reality to achieve desired outcomes.
*   Lead to ignoring relevant information to favor a preconceived viewpoint.

These pathologies, including prejudice/bias, distortions, selfishness/groupishness, fallacious thought, rationalization, and projection, enable self-deception and manifest in both egocentric and sociocentric thinking [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6].

### The Problem of Egocentric Thinking
Egocentric thinking arises because humans do not naturally consider others' rights or needs, nor the limitations of their own viewpoints. It involves using self-centered psychological standards instead of intellectual standards to determine beliefs. Common psychological standards include [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6]:
*   **Innate egocentrism:** "It's true because I believe it."
*   **Innate sociocentrism:** "It's true because we believe it."
*   **Innate wish fulfillment:** "It's true because I want to believe it."
*   **Innate self-validation:** "It's true because I have always believed it."
*   **Innate selfishness:** "It's true because it is in my best interest to believe it."

### Feelings That Accompany Egocentrism
When egocentric thinking fails to achieve its purposes, it can lead to negative feelings such as defensiveness, irritability, arrogance, anger, apathy, alienation, depression, resentment, and indifference [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6].

### The Problem of Sociocentric Thinking
Sociocentric thinking reflects an uncritical internalization of society's or culture's dominant prejudices, leading to a "culture bound" state. It includes [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6]:
*   Uncritically placing one's culture, nation, or religion above others.
*   Selecting self-serving positive descriptions for one's group and negative ones for others.
*   Internalizing group norms and beliefs without questioning.
*   Blindly conforming to group restrictions.
*   Failing to think beyond cultural prejudices.
*   Failing to internalize insights from other cultures.
*   Failing to distinguish universal ethics from cultural relativism.
*   Not recognizing how mass media shapes news from a cultural viewpoint.
*   Failing to think historically or anthropologically, thus being trapped in current thinking.
*   Failing to see it as an impediment to intellectual development.

Sociocentric thinking is diminished by cross-cultural, fairminded critical thinking [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6].

### Primary Forms of Sociocentric Thought
Four destructive forms of sociocentric thought interrelate [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6]:
1.  **Groupishness (Group Selfishness):** The tendency for groups to seek maximal benefit for themselves without regard for others' rights or needs. It is the foundational driving force behind sociocentric thinking.
2.  **Group Validation:** The tendency for groups to reinforce their own views as correct, leading to in-group thinking and judgment of outsiders based on in-group standards.
3.  **Group Control:** The tendency to ensure members conform to group expectations through enforcement, ostracism, punishment, and manipulation.
4.  **Group Conformity:** The byproduct of individuals needing to fit into groups, conforming to rules and laws set by those in control.

These forces drive the unethical pursuit of group agendas [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6].

## Envisioning Critical Societies

Cultivating rational and fair capacities in humans is essential for the emergence of critical societies. Such societies would universally value critical thinking, teach it routinely, and systematically encourage open-mindedness and intellectual virtues while discouraging closed-mindedness and egocentric/sociocentric thinking. Individuals would be encouraged to think for themselves, study irrational thought, and internalize universal intellectual standards [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6].

### 20 Barriers to Critical Societies
Many barriers impede the development of critical societies. Most people are [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6]:
1.  Only superficially aware of critical thinking.
2.  Unable to clearly articulate critical thinking ideals or habitually violate its standards.
3.  Uncritically accepting traditional cultural views.
4.  "Culture bound" within social conventions.
5.  Uncritically accepting authority figures' views.
6.  Unaware of, and not explicitly using, intellectual standards.
7.  Not understanding human thinking or its impediments.
8.  Unconsciously believing arbitrary or irrational ideas.
9.  Uncritically accepting bureaucratic rules and procedures.
10. Accepting various forms of authoritarianism.
11. Uncreative and unoriginal.
12. Trapped in their social class.
13. Unable to think well within any subject or beyond subject-matter compartments.
14. Not believing in freedom of thought and speech.
15. Biased on questions of gender, culture, species, and politics.
16. Using their intellects only superficially.
17. Having little command over primitive emotions and desires.
18. Not valuing true spontaneity or naturalness.
19. Unable/unwilling to think within different worldviews.
20. Unable to achieve self-actualization or self-command due to lack of thought command.

### Distinguishing Questions of Ethics, Social Conventions, Religion, and the Law
For fairminded critical societies, it is crucial to distinguish between ethics and other modes of thought often confused with it. While overlaps exist, understanding their dissimilarities is vital for internalizing proper ethical reasoning principles [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6]:
*   **Religious Questions (Divergent):** Deal with spirituality, subject to unlimited theological debate.
*   **Social Questions (Divergent):** Deal with customs, traditions, and taboos of groups, which vary greatly.
*   **Legal Questions (Divergent):** Deal with what is codified into law in particular societies, varying dramatically and potentially lacking an ethical basis.
*   **Ethical Questions (Convergent):** Deal with helpful or harmful behavior towards people or other creatures. Ethical principles tend to converge across cultures and groups.

Skilled ethical reasoning means developing a conscience independent of social conventions, theological systems, or unethical laws. It involves routinely applying ethical principles to genuinely ethical problems [source: es2660-the-miniature-guide-to-critical-thinking-concepts-and-tools-pdf#section-6].
